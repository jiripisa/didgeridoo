# Lips and vocal tract: how the player makes and shapes the didgeridoo sound

Scope: the lip valve, the vocal tract as the timbre control, the voice, the breath, and pitch control from the lips. Bore acoustics and musical style are left to other notes.

Reading status of the main sources (matters for how far to trust each number):

- Read in full text: Tarnopolsky et al. 2006 (JASA part I, experiment), Fletcher et al. 2006 (JASA part II, theory), Fletcher et al. 2001 (ISMA), Fletcher 1996 (Acoustics Australia, OCR text partly garbled), Wolfe & Smith 2008 (lip valve / vocal fold coupling), Wolfe & Smith 2023 review, Schneider et al. 2007, Wiggins & Storey 2010 (MRI abstract), Zolotas & Bird 2010 (ultrasound), the UNSW web pages, a web copy of Smith et al. 2007 (JASA part III).
- Abstract only: Hollenberg 2000 (full text not retrievable). Not obtained: the Nature 2005 note itself (its content is repeated in JASA part I and the UNSW pages), Wiggins 1988, Amir 2004/2005, Izdebski et al. 2012.
- Numbers that sit only in figures (lip area in mm², spectra of Fig. 10 and 11) could not be read from the text extraction and are listed under Gaps.

## 1. The lip valve: motion, waveform, source spectrum, areas, pressures, flows

### Takeaway
The lips work as an "outward-swinging door" valve that is fully closed for about half of every cycle. The measured opening-area waveform is a smooth, roughly 50 %-duty pulse whose harmonic amplitudes fall as about n⁻² (−12 dB/octave, verified only up to 500 Hz), which is darker than a sawtooth and nothing like a narrow pulse. Mouth pressure is 0.8–2 kPa and flow 0.1–0.3 L/s for the drone.

### Cited Findings
- High-speed video (1000 frames/s, front and side view through a glass-panelled square pipe) and stroboscopic schlieren images show that "the lips are closed for about half of each cycle, thus generating high levels of upper harmonics of the lip frequency". Lip frequency in these runs: period 14.3 ms (about 70 Hz). Player AT, square plexiglass pipe 1220 mm long, 38 mm internal width. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- The lip motion "conforms to the 'outward swinging door model'" (opens under excess upstream pressure, closes under excess downstream pressure), as Yoshikawa found for the low range of the horn. The playing frequency lies slightly above the first pipe resonance, as that valve type requires. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Earlier stroboscopy: didjeridu lips "normally spend an appreciable fraction of each cycle in a completely closed configuration", in contrast with a trumpet player's lips, "which normally have a nearly sinusoidal motion and just touch for a very small fraction of each cycle". The closure "enhances the harmonic content of the flow waveform ... very much what happens with the human vocal folds in speech". — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- Shape of the opening: "typically nearly elliptical", with both diameters expanding and contracting together. Idealised area function S(t) = A·⟨cos ωt − cos φ⟩ (negative values set to zero), where φ/π is the open fraction; "typically about half of the period for didjeridu players, φ ≈ π/2, though this may vary somewhat with playing style". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Measured harmonic content of the lip area (Fig. 9c of the experiment, averaged over ten cycles): "to a reasonable approximation" s_n ∝ n⁻². The difference from the idealised "transparent closure" model is ascribed to "a 'soft' closure of the lips instead of the 'sharp' closure". In the low-tongue position the harmonics from about n = 5 upward fall "even more rapidly than n⁻²". The authors suggest the player "may be able to modify this relation to some extent by changing the tension in the lip muscles". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Limit of that measurement: the 1000 frames/s camera "limits the maximum frequency in an experimental spectrum A(f) to 500 Hz" (about 7 harmonics of a 70 Hz drone). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Flow is not the area: flow through the lips follows U = [2(p_mouth − p_instrument)/ρ]^½ · S(t), and the vocal tract impedance Z_V and the instrument impedance Z_D act in series on the valve (Z = Z_V + Z_D). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Older simple model (sinusoidal lip opening that just closes once per cycle): the flow waveform "gives a spectral envelope which is initially nearly constant and then declines at about 12 dB/octave"; the transfer from flow spectrum to radiated sound "rises at 6 dB/octave at low frequencies and is then flat above about 3 kHz for the didjeridu horn". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf); the "nearly sinusoidal, just closing" assumption is contradicted by the later measurement of about half a cycle closed in [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Mouth pressure and flow (water manometer, range of loudness): "Typical mouth pressures vary from 0.8 to 2 kPa and flow rates from 0.1 to 0.3 l/s." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Earlier figures: "about 1–2 kPa (10–20 cm water gauge pressure) for the drone note and about 4–5 kPa to produce the second mode". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Model parameters used for the high-tongue calculation: blowing pressure 1 kPa, lip opening area s₀ = 50 mm² (labelled "max. area of lip opening"), playing frequency 1.03 × first tube resonance. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Sound pressure inside the mouth at comfortable playing level: about 130–145 dB re 20 µPa, varying only slightly with position in the mouth, a few dB higher on a 121 cm pipe than on a 52 cm pipe; pressure waves "with amplitudes around one kPa". One player, plastic pipes. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- An older UNSW page gives "the sound level in the mouth can be 100 dBA" (A-weighted, so not comparable with the unweighted 130–145 dB). — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- The lips radiate "roughly as loudly" backwards into the mouth as into the instrument. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- Hollenberg's time-domain simulations (abstract only): with the "approximately half-closed cycle" typical of the didjeridu, the sounding frequency of the transverse lip model is below, and of the longitudinal (swinging) model above, both the lip and the pipe resonance; significant lip closure raises the sounding frequency of the transverse model "by up to 10%" and leaves the longitudinal model "essentially unaltered"; lips sticking on closure (extra damping) "weaken significantly the generation of harmonics". — [Hollenberg 2000, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1071/PH00100)
- Jet: just after opening the jet is narrow and well defined, it broadens while the lips stay open, deviates downward, and mixes turbulently with the air in the tube. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- In the radiated sound of a cylindrical pipe the first few odd harmonics (f, 3f, 5f) are stronger than the even ones although the lip motion itself shows no such bias: that is the bore, not the source. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)

### Inferences
- Against the synthesizer's source: a sawtooth has harmonics ∝ 1/n (−6 dB/octave) and a narrow pulse a flat spectrum. The measured lip area is ∝ 1/n² (−12 dB/octave) with a duty cycle near 50 %. So the real valve is about 6 dB/octave darker than a sawtooth, and "morphing into a narrow pulse" moves away from the measurement, not towards it. A rounded half-cycle pulse (half-wave-rectified cosine with softened corners) is the closest simple shape.
- If the engine has no separate radiation stage, the +6 dB/octave radiation transfer below about 3 kHz (Fletcher 1996) on top of a −12 dB/octave flow gives a radiated envelope near −6 dB/octave, which is the sawtooth slope. A sawtooth can therefore be right for the radiated sound and wrong for the flow; the two must not be counted twice.
- "Loudness" and "lip tension" most plausibly map to how soft the closure is (how fast the harmonics fall above n ≈ 5) and to small changes of the open fraction around one half, not to a pulse-width sweep. This rests on two remarks in Fletcher et al. 2006, not on a measured series.
- From U = S·(2p/ρ)^½: at 1 kPa the jet speed is about 41 m/s, so the measured 0.1–0.3 L/s corresponds to a time-averaged opening of only about 2.5–7 mm². The model's 50 mm² is hard to reconcile with that if read as a mean; treat both as orders of magnitude.
- The acoustic pressure in the mouth (about 1 kPa amplitude) is as large as the steady blowing pressure (0.8–2 kPa). The valve therefore works in a strongly nonlinear regime; a linear source-filter model is an approximation from the start.

### Gaps
- Measured lip opening area in mm² and the open/closed timing are in Fig. 8–9 of Tarnopolsky et al. 2006 (scale bars 10 mm); the values could not be read from the text.
- No measured series of waveform or duty cycle against loudness or lip tension was found. The harmonic law is unverified above 500 Hz.
- No direct measurement of the flow waveform (only area) was found.
- Hollenberg 2000 full text (lip mass, stiffness, damping, simulated waveforms) and Wiggins 1988 (stroboscopy) were not obtained.

## 2. Vocal tract resonances and the timbre: suppressed bands and the formants between them

### Takeaway
Yes: measured during playing, maxima of the tract impedance seen at the lips (about 1.5, 2.0–2.1 and 2.8 kHz with the tongue high, 2–10 MPa·s·m⁻³) coincide with minima of the radiated envelope, typically 20 dB deep, and the audible formant (about 1.7–1.8 kHz, adjustable within roughly 1–2.2 kHz) is the band left between them, centred near a tract impedance minimum. The tract can only cut, and it does so only when the tongue is high; with the tongue low there is no formant at all, not a formant at a lower frequency.

### Cited Findings
- Method: impedance probe (8 mm × 5 mm) just inside the lips during playing, 0.2–3.0 kHz in 5.383 Hz steps, curves smoothed over 53.8 Hz; radiated sound taken on axis 125 mm from the end; cylindrical PVC pipe 1210 mm × 30 mm; three players (BL, traditional Mara player; LH, 8 years; AT). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- High tongue, lips at about 70 Hz: "The vocal tract impedance shows broad peaks at approximately 1.5, 2.1, and 2.8 kHz". The 1.5 kHz peak is "about 8 MPa s m⁻³"; the pipe's own impedance peaks in this range are 3–10 MPa·s·m⁻³. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Conclusion values: tract impedance maxima of "2–10 MPa s m⁻³ in the range 1 to 3 kHz—comparable with or larger than a typical impedance maximum of the yidaki in that frequency range". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- The resulting formant: "a strong formant between about 1.5 and 2 kHz, whose frequency and amplitude may be varied by the performer"; clear formants "near 1.7 kHz" in Figs. 4 and 6 (about 1.6 kHz in Fig. 6). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Same result in the group's words: impedance peaks "coincide with antiformants at about 1.5 and 2.0 kHz, leaving a prominent formant ... at about 1.8 kHz"; "peaks in |Z_tract| create antiformants and minima in |Z_tract| correspond to formants". — [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Statistics: sound-envelope extrema between 1.0 and 2.2 kHz against the nearest impedance extrema give slope 0.93 and correlation coefficient 0.98. "The impedance maxima correspond almost exactly to minima in the spectral envelope of the sound, while maxima in the sound spectrum occur on average at frequencies slightly above those of the minimum in the impedance." No clear impedance maxima were evident for player BL (the player who found the probe in his mouth disruptive). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); "46 measurements on three players" — [UNSW yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidaki.html)
- Depth: sufficiently high tract impedance peaks "inhibit acoustic airflow entering the instrument at those frequencies, by typically 20 dB (Tarnopolsky et al., 2006, Figs. 4 and 6)". The tract's main influence is "formants in the radiated sound in the frequency range from about 1 to 2 kHz". — [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html)
- Threshold: the tract matters when its impedance is "comparable to or greater than that of the instrument"; highest-ranked instruments have Z ≤ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 2 kHz, lowest-ranked Z ≥ 3 MPa·s·m⁻³; players "can produce resonances of the vocal tract with impedances of a few megohms". — [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html); [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- Width: tract resonances are "much broader than the frequency differences between the harmonics of the lip vibration", so "several harmonics of the played sound will fall within a formant"; the 1–2 kHz formants "typically involve the 20 to 30th harmonics". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html)
- Theory (first-order): harmonics "lying close to impedance maxima of the vocal tract are suppressed, so that formant bands appear near impedance minima of the vocal tract". Model tract: two series resonances (impedance minima) at 1.5 and 2.5 kHz, Q = 10, characteristic impedance 4 MPa·s·m⁻³, plus a glottis/lung branch. Result: "two pronounced formants in the radiated sound spectrum ... their center frequencies coincide with those of the two minima in the vocal-tract impedance", and "a pronounced minimum at the frequency of the maximum in the tract impedance". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Low tongue: impedance peaks "not as large", "none of the shaping of the spectral envelope", "no clear formant comparable to those near 1.7 kHz"; only the strong low harmonics rise above the turbulent noise in the mouth signal. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- A second, weaker source of bands, independent of the tract: "harmonic coincidence", where a group of odd or even harmonics lands on bore resonances (odd harmonics raised around 2.5 kHz in the low-tongue example; even harmonics around 1.5 kHz in Fig. 6). A change of playing frequency of "only a couple of percent" switches it on or off. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html)
- What stays constant: below about 300 Hz hearing is insensitive, so "the fundamental and lower harmonics are not very loud. Further, they vary little during playing"; the formants lie where the ear is most sensitive and carry "most of the interest". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Relation to speech: the radiated formants "correspond approximately to those of the second formant frequency produced in speech with the same tract configuration", "comparable with, but not equal to" F2, because in playing the lips are almost shut. The first speech formant ("below about 1 kHz for all vowels") is not the one involved. "Some vowel shapes with low tongue may not produce a clear formant." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Speech reference from the same lab: tracts saying "heed" have a strong resonance at about 1.8 kHz; "hoard" or "hot" have none between 1 and 2 kHz. — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- Earlier spectra: a formant band "at about 1500 Hz", moved "to about 2.2 kHz" by reducing the mouth volume; "some evidence for a lower vocal-tract formant at about 500 Hz"; estimated tunable range of the mouth resonance "about 500 Hz to about 3 kHz". The picture then was the mouth as a Helmholtz resonator, vented by the lip opening and heavily damped by it, so that its bandwidth "encompasses the frequencies of several harmonics" whose flow is enhanced. — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Mimed-playing measurements gave tract impedance maxima "in the range 1.5–2.5 kHz" for the constricted ("nasal") sound, and the paper said that "formant resonances in the sound are associated with impedance maxima". — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf); the association is contradicted by the measurement during playing (maxima go with minima of the sound) in [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- A weak tract impedance peak below 500 Hz is often seen when players mime, but not during playing (possibly masked by the lip signal). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- In one recording the fifth harmonic of the drone (a seventeenth above it) is strongly emphasised by a tract resonance near that frequency. — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)

### Inferences
- Linearising the paper's flow equation gives, for harmonic n, u_n = u_n(no load) / (1 + Z_n/R_lip), with Z_n = Z_V + Z_D at that frequency and R_lip = (2ρp₀)^½ / s₀. With p₀ = 1 kPa and s₀ = 50 mm², R_lip ≈ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³. A resistive peak of 2, 4, 8 and 10 MPa·s·m⁻³ then cuts the flow by about 10, 14, 19 and 21 dB. That reproduces the reported "typically 20 dB" for an 8 MPa·s·m⁻³ peak and explains why instruments below 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ are preferred. (My derivation from equations 16–23 of Fletcher et al. 2006; the extracted text of those equations is damaged.)
- Because Z is passive, |1 + Z/R_lip| ≥ 1: in this model the tract can only attenuate. A formant is the band of least attenuation, never a boost above the unloaded source. This is the essential difference from speech, where a resonance raises the level by its Q.
- For the synthesizer this means: two band-pass filters added to the dry signal produce peaks on an untouched floor; the instrument produces two (or three) notches about 20 dB deep with a pass band between them. The audible result differs most in the regions around 1.5 and 2.0–2.1 kHz, which should drop well below the "no vowel" level when the vowel is strong.
- Geometry of the measured high-tongue pattern: notch centres about 600–700 Hz apart (1.5, 2.1, 2.8 kHz), so the pass band holds roughly 7–9 harmonics of a 70 Hz drone. The model's Q = 10 corresponds to resonance widths of 150 Hz at 1.5 kHz and 250 Hz at 2.5 kHz.
- Against the five-point speech table (F1 300–700 Hz, F2 650–2250 Hz): the documented, controllable band is F2-like and lives at about 1–2.2 kHz (up to about 3 kHz for the next one). The F1 band is at best weak ("some evidence ... at about 500 Hz"). The measured counterpart of the open vowels (the "aw" of hoard or hot, low tongue) is the absence of a formant, not an F2 gliding down to 650–1100 Hz. No measurement exists for an "oo" shape during playing. A table entry with F2 below about 1 kHz therefore has no measured counterpart.
- Formant strength should scale with a "tract impedance" control (tongue height and glottis closure), fading to nothing, rather than staying constant while only the frequency moves.

### Gaps
- No tabulated bandwidths or depths per player; the 20 dB figure is the authors' own reading of two figures.
- No measurement of how fast players sweep the band (glide times), and none for vowel shapes other than "high" and "low" tongue.
- Tract impedance below 200 Hz and above 3 kHz was not measured.
- The Nature 2005 note itself was not read; it used a traditional instrument, and its numbers are known here only through the JASA paper and the UNSW pages.

## 3. Tongue and mouth shapes, the glottis and the cheeks

### Takeaway
Only two shapes are measured: "high tongue" (like "ee" in heed; passage about 10 mm in diameter; strong band at 1.5–2 kHz) and "low tongue" (like "aw"; open, uniform tract; no formant). Strong formants also need a nearly closed glottis (about 20 mm² opening); an open glottis lets the lungs damp the resonances. Everything else (named vowels, syllables) is player description without frequencies.

### Cited Findings
- High tongue: tongue close to the hard palate, a constriction between throat and lips; "very common in yidaki performance". Low tongue: "no lingual constriction", "a sound without a strong formant ... used as a contrast to the high-tongue drone". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- MRI of a player (LH) during playing: uninflected drone, tract "open" and of roughly uniform width from lips to vocal folds; "nasal"-quality sound, tongue raised to constrict the tract between the lips and the soft palate. — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- High-tongue geometry: tongue raised along its whole length and brought close to the teeth, "a narrow passage of effective diameter about 10 mm from the root of the tongue up to the lips"; jaw and tongue can turn the passage from nearly cylindrical to nearly conical, which moves the resonances. Low tongue: the mouth "simply extends the trachea into a uniform tube up to the lips" (trachea about 20 mm in diameter), giving low impedance. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- How it sounds: alternating high and low tongue is "a little like someone alternating between the sound 'ee' (the vowel in the English word 'heed') and an indistinct vowel something like 'aw' (the vowel in 'hoard' or 'hot')"; the pitch changes along with the timbre. — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- With the tongue very near the roof of the mouth the player inhibits "the frequencies around 1.5 and 2.0 kHz", leaving "a strong isolated band around 1.8 kHz that gives the instrument a sound a bit like 'ee'". — [UNSW didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- Reducing the mouth volume ("constricting the mouth") raised the band from about 1.5 kHz to about 2.2 kHz. — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Glottis: wind players perform "with the glottis almost closed" (Mukai); a nearly closed glottis gives a large reflection coefficient and "relatively strong resonances", while an open one ends in the lungs, "essentially resistive", and gives "rather weak resonances". Learning to play with the glottis nearly closed "may be important in developing technique". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Numbers for the adducted glottis: opening about 2 × 10 mm (20 mm²), effective thickness about 5 mm, inertive impedance about 2–6 MPa·s·m⁻³ at 1–3 kHz, against about 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ for the trachea and about 2 MPa·s·m⁻³ (resistive) for the lungs below it. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- The useful glottis position is "something like the position used for whispering"; an artificial player with a controllable "glottis" shows weaker resonances and weaker formants when it is open. Players are not aware of using it. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html); [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html); [UNSW didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- With the folds in the whisper position and no intent to sing, the sound in the mouth drives the vocal folds at the drone frequency with an amplitude comparable to deliberate singing. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- Cheeks: during the inhalation only "the closed volume of the mouth" stands behind the lips instead of the tube down to the vocal folds, with "very different resonances". — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- Articulation (acoustician's description): the pulsing drone is decorated "by tongue vibrations, so that the player effectively says unvoiced words such as 'ritoru' or even 'didjeridu', with the final 'u' sound prolonged". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Player-teacher description (Yolŋu style): two tongue positions, retroflexed (tip curled back against the gum ridge) and interdental (tip between the teeth). "dith-dhu" thrusts from retroflexed to interdental with a pulse of air from the belly and gives "a very brief hint at the trumpeted note"; "dhirrl" pulls back, flicking the gum ridge, with "a strong pulse of air and often a breath in". Non-Yolŋu players use double and triple tonguing ("taka taka"). — [Yidaki story, tongue motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/tongue-motions/)
- MRI confirms the interdental technique: the tongue is "thrust forward between the teeth and allowed to vibrate against the lower lip", then retracted, "creating a distinct pulse in the sound". — [Wiggins & Storey 2010](https://cds.ismrm.org/protected/10MProceedings/PDFfiles/5007_7323.PDF)
- Ultrasound of one player (3 years' experience), five articulations: "undulation" (tongue tip at the alveolar ridge, body moving back through post-alveolar, palatal and velar positions, cyclic); "piston" (tongue strongly retracted, then shot forward to fill the mouth, a high-amplitude burst); "alveolar trill" (the effect "can sound like a growl, and this is often what it is called"); alveolar and velar ejectives (closure in the mouth and at the glottis, larynx raised, then release). No acoustic analysis is given. — [Zolotas & Bird 2010](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/view/2313)

### Inferences
- The "undulation" is a constriction travelling from front to back, so it should sweep the 1–2.2 kHz band; it is the nearest documented gesture to a vowel glide (wee-oo). No frequencies are published for it.
- A vowel control is better thought of as two quantities: where the constriction sits (band frequency, about 1.5 to 2.2 kHz) and how tight it is together with how closed the glottis is (band depth, zero to about 20 dB).
- "Growl" is ambiguous among players: it can mean a tongue-tip trill, an amplitude and timbre flutter at trill rate, as well as voice. The synthesizer's growl models only the voice.

### Gaps
- No measured frequencies for named shapes (ee, oo, ah, "wok", "ritoru" and the like); "wok" was not found in any source read.
- No measurement of what inflated cheeks do to the tract resonances during normal blowing.
- Izdebski et al. 2012 (endoscopy of the glottis during playing) was found only as a citation; its results were not read.

## 4. Vocalisations: singing, growls and calls while droning

### Takeaway
Lips and vocal folds are two valves in series on the same air stream, so the flow is the product of two pulse-like openings and contains every frequency m·g ± n·f. Singing a fifth (3:2) or, "more usually", a tenth (5:2) above the drone gives a subharmonic series on f/2; a fourth gives f/3 and a major third f/4. The difference tone itself is weak in the radiated sound; the low pitch is heard from its harmonics.

### Cited Findings
- Mechanism: both valves "'clip' the total current when they close"; with lip area and glottal area each a harmonic series, flow ∝ S_lips · S_glottis, giving "components at all frequencies nω₁ ± mω₂"; the dominant terms have small m and n. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf); [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Measured example (player LH): drone f = 70 Hz, voice g = 3f/2 = 105 Hz; all components are equally spaced at f/2 = g/3 = 35 Hz. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Intervals used: "a musical fifth (frequency ratio 3:2) or more usually a musical tenth (frequency ratio 5:2) above the drone fundamental"; the difference term (m = n = 1, or m = 2, n = 1) "generates an impressive subharmonic drone of frequency ω_D/2, together with all its harmonics". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- For the tenth: "There is not much radiated energy in this sub-octave fundamental, but the subjective pitch is generated strongly from the sequence of harmonics", with a rough, pulsating quality. — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Electrode study (one non-expert player, JW, plastic pipes): f ≈ 71 Hz with g ≈ 106 Hz (fifth, 121 cm × 34 mm pipe); f ≈ 155 Hz with g ≈ 206 Hz (fourth) and f ≈ 164 Hz with g ≈ 205 Hz (major third) on a 52 cm × 26 mm pipe. Difference tones: f/2, f/3 (a twelfth below) and f/4 (two octaves below). For f = 72 Hz these are 36 and 24 Hz. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf); [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Level of the difference tone: "The difference frequency g − f itself is rather weak in the sound signal, in part because lower frequencies are less well radiated"; the published spectra are magnified ten times below f to show it. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- Mutual coupling: the heterodyne components appear in the lip motion and in the vocal fold motion as well as in the sound. For the fifth, the ratio of the f and g components is inverted between lips and folds; for the fourth and third the lips' influence on the folds is "much stronger than the reverse", and the player never managed the opposite. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- Voice strength in the mouth: singing "oo" to match the pipe's output gave 136–140 dB in the mouth, humming about 140–145 dB, against 130–145 dB from the lips alone. — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- Voice close to the drone pitch: "very strong 'beats'"; in the oscillograms the two signals are of comparable size, then the player locks the voice onto the lips. — [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf); [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)
- Glides: when the voice glides, "the heterodyne frequencies also glide, some in either direction". — [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Range: a vocal third above a 71 Hz drone (about F♯2) was too low to sing strongly; players prefer instruments from about A1 to F2, where "for typical male voices, pitches in the middle of this range suit the heterodyne effects". — [Wolfe & Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf); [UNSW didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- The tract formant acts on the voice partials too: in the fifth example the fifth harmonic of the drone is heard strongly. — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- Animal calls ("barking dingos, brolgas and other animals"): the voice pitch is "rather high so that frequency mixing does not have such a pronounced effect", and the calls stay recognisable. Most such sounds are "complex and transient". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf); [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Usage: "Vocalisations are much more common in modern styles than in traditional performance." — [UNSW didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- Player-teacher description (Yolŋu): players "add a growly quality to the tone with subtle use of voice along with the rhythm", "almost like grunting along with the 'dhirrl'"; bird calls such as the brolga belong to particular songs; no sung melodies over the drone. — [Yidaki story, adding voice](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/adding-voice/)

### Inferences
- Ring-modulating the lip source with a sine at g produces only n·f ± g and removes the plain harmonics n·f. The physical product is of two openings that never go negative, each with a steady part and many harmonics: (a₀ + Σ aₙ cos nft)·(b₀ + Σ bₘ cos mgt). It keeps the drone harmonics, adds the voice harmonics m·g, and adds all m·g ± n·f. Amplitude modulation by a pulse-like voice wave with depth below 100 % is the closer model.
- The interval control should favour 3:2 and 5:2 (subharmonic at f/2), then 4:3 (f/3) and 5:4 (f/4), plus unison with a few hertz of detuning for beats. With a 70 Hz drone the voice sits at 105 Hz (fifth) or 175 Hz (tenth).
- Because the product is formed before the lips, the tract notches and the bore act on the voice and heterodyne partials as they do on the drone.
- The Yolŋu "growl" is a short, rhythm-locked grunt, not a sustained tone at an interval; the sustained fifth or tenth is the contemporary technique.

### Gaps
- No published levels (dB) of the voice partials or heterodyne components relative to the drone; the figures could not be read numerically, the only measured player was not an expert, and the authors expect experts to be louder.
- No acoustic analysis of shrieks, dingo or bird calls, or of the trill "growl" (rate, depth).
- No data on how accurately players hold the interval or how they glide.

## 5. Breath and noise; circular breathing

### Takeaway
The inhalation is short, typically 0.2 s, and during it the sound continues on cheek air with lower and falling amplitude and a different timbre, because only the closed mouth stands behind the lips. No measurement of breath noise level or spectrum was found; the papers mention turbulence only in passing.

### Cited Findings
- Duration: "the consequent brief period during inhalation (typically 0.2 s for this player)". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Loudness: in a common rhythm of three inhalations, "The amplitude falls during the inhalations and is largest during normal playing", and it decreases within each inhalation, explained by the Young–Laplace relation (flatter cheeks give less pressure for the same muscle tension). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Capacity and pattern: cheek air "supports loud playing for only a fraction of a second"; simple alternation allows "only shallow breathing"; players often blow "for several seconds" from the lungs and then refill "during a series of three or four alternations" of brief inhalation and normal playing, especially around high-flow passages such as overblown notes. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Timbre: the change of tract geometry "makes a substantial change in the timbre", "usually unavoidable", and is built into the rhythm; the tract impedance during the inhalation could not be measured because it is too short. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Sequence seen by MRI (sagittal FLASH, listed TR 120 ms): soft palate opens, tongue presses against it and traps air over the tongue and in the cheeks, then "the tongue moves forward like a piston to force air out of the mouth into the instrument" while air is inhaled through the nose. — [Wiggins & Storey 2010](https://cds.ismrm.org/protected/10MProceedings/PDFfiles/5007_7323.PDF)
- Sequence seen by ultrasound: cheeks extended first; tongue moves back until it blocks the back of the mouth; cheeks tense; then "the tongue then acts as a syringe", pushing air out fast enough to keep the lips sounding. — [Zolotas & Bird 2010](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/view/2313)
- The breathing adds "a pulsation to the drone", then "enhanced by rhythmic variations in blowing pressure and vocal tract configuration". The instrument "uses air at rather a high rate". — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf); [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Player-teacher description: Yolŋu players take "lots of small breaths" driven by "bursts of air from the belly" and keep the cheeks in ("not a big, sudden cheek squeeze"); the quick inhalation comes at the end of the "dhirrl" tongue motion; Western players call this "bounce breathing", and otherwise use the cheeks as a bellows and may take one large breath after a longer stretch. — [Yidaki story, breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)
- Noise, indirect evidence only: the jet "experiences viscous drag ... which leads to turbulent mixing"; with the tongue low only the strong low harmonics are "clearly visible above the turbulent noise present inside the mouth"; an artificial player with a simple spring lip is "rather breathy" from turbulence around the valve. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [UNSW sound files page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didj_files.html)
- Broadband sound injected into the mouth and leaking through the lips into the pipe comes out with "an envelope that resembles the inverse of the vocal tract impedance". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)

### Inferences
- A breath event for the synthesizer: about 0.2 s; level steps down and then sags further; the vowel band changes abruptly to a "mouth only" setting and returns. In Western style expect one such event after several seconds or a burst of three or four; in Yolŋu style a small one in nearly every rhythmic cell.
- Air used per inhalation: 0.2 s at 0.1–0.3 L/s is about 20–60 mL from the cheeks and mouth.
- Noise that originates upstream of the lips (glottis, tongue constriction) passes the same lip valve and should carry the tract's notch pattern, as the injected test noise did; it is also chopped by the lips, which are shut half the time, so it is modulated at the drone frequency. Adding steady pink noise before the vowel filters gets the colouring roughly right but misses the modulation. Noise from the jet inside the tube bypasses the tract and is coloured by the bore only.
- Pink is an assumption: nothing found says what the noise spectrum is.

### Gaps
- No measured breath-noise level relative to the harmonics, no noise spectrum, no dependence on loudness.
- No statistics of inhalation rate or duration across players (one player, one figure).
- No measured pitch change during the inhalation.
- The spectra of normal playing and inhalation (Fig. 10) could not be read numerically.

## 6. Pitch control from the lips

### Takeaway
The drone normally sits 2–3 % above the first bore resonance, and the lips can move it by only about a semitone in total; players tend to raise it when stressing formants, and tongue position shifts it slightly. No measurements of vibrato, slow drift or onset pitch were found.

### Cited Findings
- Across 38 instruments: "The average difference between the frequency of the nominal playing pitch and f₁ was 2%, with the pitch frequency usually slightly above f₁"; one anomalous instrument played at 98 Hz between f₁ = 102 Hz and f₂/3 = 92 Hz. — [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html)
- On the PVC pipe (1210 mm, lips at about 70 Hz in the companion example) the playing frequency was "slightly above that of the first resonance—about 3 Hz in this case". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Half a semitone (3 %) above the resonance is "fairly characteristic of actual playing in the case of emphasized formants"; the model uses 1.03. "Informal observations of competent didjeridu players show that they typically raise the drone frequency when wishing to emphasize formants." — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Range: the drone is a little above the resonance for an outward-swinging valve and a little below for a sliding one; the player can move between them, but "the total range of variation in the case of the didjeridu is only about a semitone (6%)". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Direction: "lipping down and to a less extent lipping up are relatively easy on the instrument, as the model of Hollenberg (2000) predicts". — [Wolfe & Smith 2023](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Simulation: lip closure raises the sounding frequency of the transverse lip model "by up to 10%" and leaves the swinging model unchanged. — [Hollenberg 2000, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1071/PH00100)
- Tongue: "the pitch as well as the timbre is changed by the tongue position". — [UNSW yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- Small pitch moves change the timbre: at the 20th–30th harmonic a change of "only a couple of percent (a fraction of a semitone)" moves a harmonic from a bore impedance peak to a dip; the quality study allowed players ± one semitone. A very skilled player can tune the drone so that one harmonic is strongly enhanced, "though this technique is not used in traditional playing". — [Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html); [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Typical drone: "frequency usually about 70 Hz", range 55–80 Hz. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Overblowing needs a much higher lip tension and blowing pressure (4–5 kPa against 1–2 kPa). — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)

### Inferences
- A pitch-bend range of about ±0.5 semitone around a rest point 2–3 % above the bore resonance covers what is documented, with more room downward than upward. Larger bends have no support in these sources.
- Coupling the vowel control to pitch is realistic: a strong (high-tongue) vowel goes with a drone raised by up to about 3 %, roughly 50 cents.
- Since a 2 % pitch change reshuffles which upper harmonics meet bore resonances, a small bend should audibly change the brightness between 1 and 2 kHz even with the vowel fixed. That only happens if the bore resonances in that range are modelled.

### Gaps
- No measured vibrato rate or depth, no slow drift over a breath, no pitch trajectory at note onset or during the inhalation.
- No measured maximum downward bend. Lesson pages by player-teachers describe a "jaw drop" that lowers the drone, without intervals; those pages were seen only as search excerpts and not read.
- Hollenberg 2000 full text (frequency against lip resonance curves) was not obtained.
