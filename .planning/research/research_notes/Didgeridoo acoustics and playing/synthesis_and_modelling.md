# Synthesis and modelling of the didgeridoo (didjeridu, yidaki): prior art

Source classes used below: **[PR]** peer-reviewed journal paper, **[CONF]** conference paper, **[CODE]** published source code, **[HOBBY]** forum post, blog or hobby patch, **[MKT]** vendor page or marketing copy, **[JOURN]** magazine article.

Reading note on equations: the three JASA papers and the 1996 Acoustics Australia paper were read from text extractions of the PDFs (`pdftotext`). The extraction drops Greek letters and some operators, so the equations below were restored from context (and, for the flared-tube formula, re-derived). Parameter values and prose statements are as printed. Check any equation against the PDF before coding it.

## 1. Physical models of the didgeridoo in the literature

### Takeaway
There is exactly one worked-out physical model family: the ANU / UNSW / Melbourne line (Fletcher 1983, 1996; Hollenberg 2000; Fletcher et al. 2006), which treats lungs, glottis, vocal tract, lip valve and bore as one series circuit and shows that each harmonic of the lip flow is divided by (lip resistance + tract impedance + bore impedance). No published real-time waveguide didgeridoo synthesizer, no Kob model and no Tamara Smyth didgeridoo model were found; the only published code is hobby-level.

### Cited Findings

#### Fletcher, Hollenberg, Smith, Tarnopolsky, Wolfe 2006, "II. Theory" [PR] (the model behind the Nature 2005 result)
- Structure: an electrical analog network (voltage = pressure, current = volume flow) of lungs, glottis, vocal tract, lips and tube; ultimately the tract and the tube are reduced to two input impedances, Z_V just inside the lips and Z_D just outside, "on the two sides of the lip valve", i.e. in series for the flow U through the lips. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Lungs: modelled as a branching tubule tree (diameter and length ratio 0.8 per branching, 16 orders, about 1 litre of tubules plus 3 litres of alveolar volume); near 70 Hz and from about 0.5 to 3 kHz the subglottal impedance is almost purely resistive, about 2 x 10^6 Pa s m^-3 (the characteristic impedance of the bronchus). In the network it is a DC pressure source in series with a large inertance, a compliance and a resistance. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Glottis: a series inertance. For "typically adducted" folds the opening is about 2 x 10 mm and the effective thickness about 5 mm, giving an impedance of about 300·ω Pa s m^-3, i.e. about 2 to 6 MPa s m^-3 between 1 and 3 kHz; compare trachea characteristic impedance ρc/S ≈ 1 MPa s m^-3 and lung resistance ≈ 2 MPa s m^-3. A well-adducted glottis therefore isolates the upper-tract resonances from lung damping. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Vocal tract: trachea about 20 mm in diameter; in the "high tongue" configuration the tongue forms a passage of effective diameter about 10 mm from the root of the tongue to the lips, which "greatly increases" Z_V; in the "low tongue" position the mouth just extends the trachea as a uniform tube, giving a low Z_V. The simplified Z_V used for calculation is two series-resonant LRC circuits in parallel (the tract resonances, i.e. impedance minima) plus a parallel L-R branch for glottis and lungs. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Bore, cylinder (eq. 3-4): Z11 = Z22 = -j·Z0·cot(kL), Z12 = Z21 = -j·Z0·csc(kL), Z0 = 4ρc/(π d^2), k = ω/c - jα, α ≈ 10^-5 · β · ω^(1/2) / d in SI units, β ≈ 2.4 for perfectly smooth walls and "much larger" for a naturally rough didjeridu. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Bore, flaring truncated cone (eq. 5), with θ1 = arctan(k·x1), θ2 = arctan(k·x2), x1 and x2 the distances of the two ends from the cone apex: Z11 = -(4jρc/(π d1^2)) · sin(kL+θ2)·sin θ1 / sin(kL+θ2-θ1); Z22 = +(4jρc/(π d2^2)) · sin(kL-θ1)·sin θ2 / sin(kL+θ2-θ1); Z12 = Z21 = -(4jρc/(π d1 d2)) · sin θ1·sin θ2 / sin(kL+θ2-θ1). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Mode frequencies of a slightly flared tube (eq. 2; same formula in the 1996 paper): ω_n ≈ (n - 1/2)·(πc/2L')·{1 + [1 + 4(d2 - d1)/(π^2 d1 (n - 1/2)^2)]^(1/2)}, with L' = L + 0.3·d2. For d2 = d1 this is the odd series 1, 3, 5… x c/4L'; for a complete cone 2, 4, 6… x c/4L'; in between "a stretched quasiharmonic sequence". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf); [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Radiation impedance at the open end (eq. 6, SI units): Z_R ≈ 7 x 10^-4·ω^2 + 0.5j·ρω/d2 when k·d2 < 1, and ≈ ρc/A2 ≈ 500/d2^2 when k·d2 > 1. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Lip valve, measured kinematics used as input: lips are open for only about half the cycle, the opening is nearly elliptical, and the area is approximately S(t) = A·<cos ωt - cos φ> (negative values set to zero), φ/π being the open fraction, typically φ ≈ π/2 ("transparent closure" model). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Lip-area spectrum: with S(t) = Σ s_n cos nωt, stroboscopic measurement gives s_n ≈ s_0 / n^2 "to a reasonable approximation" (the idealised model is said to predict n^-1; the difference is ascribed to soft closure). In the low-tongue case harmonics above n = 5 fall "even more rapidly than n^-2". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Lip dynamics (eq. 9), one degree of freedom: m·z'' + (damping)·z' + m·ω0^2·(z - z0) = A·(σ1·p_M + σ2·p_D), z = lip opening, m = combined effective lip mass, A = effective lip area, p_M mouth pressure, p_D pressure at the tube input, σ1 = +1 and σ2 = +1 or -1 depending on whether the valve is "sliding door" (+,+) or "outward-swinging door" (+,-). No numerical values for m, damping or ω0 are given. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Playing frequency: linear theory puts the drone "a little above the didjeridu resonance" for a (+,-) valve and a little below for a (+,+) valve; the total range of variation is "only about a semitone (6%)"; competent players "typically raise the drone frequency when wishing to emphasize formants". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Flow equations (eq. 14-17): p_L - p_M = Z_V·U; p_D = Z_D·U; U = [2(p_M - p_D)/ρ]^(1/2)·S(t); hence U = (2/ρ)^(1/2)·[p_L - (Z_V + Z_D)·U]^(1/2)·S(t). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Full solution method proposed but NOT carried out: harmonic balance (assume p_M, ω0 and amplitude; compute flow by Bernoulli; Fourier transform; get pressures; recompute lip motion; iterate). "Such a detailed computation will not be carried out or discussed further at this time." — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- First-order result actually used (eq. 20-23), with Z_n = R_n + jX_n the value of Z_V + Z_D at the n-th harmonic, p0 the steady mouth pressure and s_0 the mean lip area: flow magnitude of harmonic n is |U_n| = 2·p0·s_n / {[(2ρp0)^(1/2) + s_0·R_n]^2 + s_0^2·X_n^2}^(1/2). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Transfer to the radiated sound (eq. 26-27): flow out of the open end U_2n ≈ [Z21/(Z22 + Z_R)]·U_1n ≈ (Z21/Z22)·U_1n, which "has maxima at the same frequencies as does the input impedance at the lip end"; radiated power Π_n = R_R(nω)·|U_2n|^2. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Table I, "Model parameter values (high tongue)": tube length L = 1.3 m; tube diameter d1 = 40 mm; loss coefficient for tube β = 12; first vocal-tract resonance 1.5 kHz; second 2.5 kHz; Q of vocal-tract resonances 10; characteristic impedance (L/C)^(1/2) = 4 MPa s m^-3; glottal open area 20 mm^2; effective glottal length 5 mm; blowing pressure in mouth p0 = 1 kPa; maximum area of lip opening s_0 = 50 mm^2; playing frequency relative to first resonance 1.03. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- What it reproduces: two pronounced formants centred on the two minima of Z_V and a pronounced minimum at the Z_V maximum, agreeing "very well" with measured high-tongue spectra on a cylindrical pipe; odd harmonics dominate at low frequency, and because the drone is 3% above the first resonance the emphasis transfers to even harmonics "at about 1000 Hz" and back "at about 3500 Hz". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Stated limits: the first-order result "will not be accurate at low frequencies"; conclusions "are certainly not quantitatively accurate"; "the model still requires development if it is to give an accurate quantitative description of the radiated spectrum". Lip motion is assumed, not computed. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Flaring bore: results "generally similar", but there are no clear odd / even regions, and the low harmonics above the fundamental are weaker because they do not sit on tube resonances. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- No code is published with the paper. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)

#### Tarnopolsky et al. 2006, "I. Experiment" [PR] (numbers the model depends on)
- Test instruments: cylindrical PVC pipe, length 1210 mm, inner diameter 30 mm; and a square-section plexiglass pipe 1220 mm long, internal width 38 mm, for optical work. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- High tongue: tract impedance shows broad peaks at approximately 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz; the 1.5 kHz peak is about 8 MPa s m^-3; conclusion gives tract maxima of 2 to 10 MPa s m^-3 in 1 to 3 kHz, "comparable with or larger than" a typical bore maximum there (the pipe has peaks of 3 to 10 MPa s m^-3 in that range). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Spectral minima of the radiated envelope vs. nearest tract impedance maxima, and spectral maxima vs. nearest impedance minima (range 1.0 to 2.2 kHz, three players): slope 0.93, correlation coefficient 0.98; sound maxima sit on average slightly above the impedance minima. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- High tongue produces "a strong formant between about 1.5 and 2 kHz, whose frequency and amplitude may be varied by the performer"; it corresponds approximately to the second speech formant F2 of the same tract shape, "similar to but not necessarily equal". Low tongue: no clear formant; tract impedance low across the range. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Signal in the mouth shows no strong odd / even difference, because tract resonances are much broader than the harmonic spacing; several harmonics fall inside one formant. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- "Harmonic coincidence": weak, broad formants can also arise when groups of even or odd harmonics coincide with bore resonances (the playing frequency was about 3 Hz above the first resonance in one example; around 1.5 kHz the even harmonics coincide). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Lips: lip period 14.3 ms (about 70 Hz); closed for about half of each cycle; motion conforms to the "outward swinging door" model; lip-area harmonics fall as about n^-2 (their Fig. 9c). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Typical mouth pressures 0.8 to 2 kPa and flow rates 0.1 to 0.3 l/s. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Jet: emerges narrow at opening and broadens; "turbulent mixing because of the high speed of the jet"; for sound generation what matters is "the input volume flow at a particular frequency multiplied by the instrument impedance at that frequency". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)

#### Fletcher 1996, Acoustics Australia [PR, review-style]
- Simple lip-flow model: lip opening x = a0 + a·sin(2πft), U ≈ γ·x·(p0 - p)^(1/2) (eq. 3), with the mouthpiece pressure p ≈ R·U, R being the acoustic resistance of the tube at the resonance; lip motion is nearly sinusoidal because the valve operates very near its own resonance, and the lips "just about close once in each cycle". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- That flow waveform has a spectral envelope "initially nearly constant" that "then declines at about 12 dB/octave"; the flow-to-radiation transfer "rises at 6 dB/octave at low frequencies and is then flat above about 3 kHz"; the constant-R assumption is "not very good". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Earlier explanation of formants (superseded in detail by 2006): mouth cavity plus lip opening as a heavily damped Helmholtz resonator, tunable "from about 500 Hz to about 3 kHz"; example formants at about 1500 Hz and about 2.2 kHz, with "some evidence for a lower vocal-tract formant at about 500 Hz". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Blowing pressure about 1 to 2 kPa for the drone and about 4 to 5 kPa for the second mode; the second mode needs much higher lip tension and is used "only for brief accents"; a sounded upper mode carries its own harmonic series. — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- A detailed vibrating-lip model like those for vocal folds "has not yet been attempted". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)

#### Hollenberg 2000, Aust. J. Phys. 53(6), 835-850 [PR] (abstract level only)
- Time-domain simulation of transverse and longitudinal lip-vibration models coupled to the input impedance function of a straight pipe, focused on strongly non-sinusoidal lip motion with the lips closed for about half the cycle. The transverse model sounds below the lip and pipe resonances, the longitudinal model above (in line with linear theory); significant lip closure raises the transverse model's sounding frequency by up to about 10% and leaves the longitudinal one essentially unchanged; modelling lips that stick (stronger damping on closure) substantially weakens harmonic generation in both. — [Hollenberg 2000, abstract](https://www.publish.csiro.au/PH/PH00100)

#### Smith, Rey, Dickens, Fletcher, Hollenberg, Wolfe 2007, "III. Determinants of playing quality" [PR] (bore parameter ranges)
- 38 traditional didjeridus and 11 PVC pipes, 7 players; ranked quality correlates negatively with input-impedance magnitude, particularly from 1 to 2 kHz: good instruments have "little structure and only maxima of low magnitude" there. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Measured ranges: effective characteristic impedance Z0 about 0.2 to 1 MPa s m^-3 (equivalent to cylinders of about 25 to 50 mm); first maximum Z1 about 5 to 40 MPa s m^-3; Z1/Z0 about 20 to 70; Q of first resonance 12 to 86; f1 about 50 to 105 Hz; f2/f1 about 2.6 to 3.0; playing pitch on average 2% above f1. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

#### Derived and hobby models
- Hindle and Posnett, NIME 2017 [CONF]: an electro-acoustic instrument (a speaker in a 4th-order bandpass box driving a real tube) excited by dijj(f, a, t) = p0/R - (p0^2/R^3)/(a0 + a·sin(2πft))^2, attributed to Fletcher 1996; f = lip frequency, 0 ≤ a < a0 lip opening, R = tube resistance at resonance. Their own assessment: few harmonics when a is near 0, many as a approaches a0, but it "replicates very little of the higher formants", has "far less variation" than a human, and "the high frequencies roll off sharply after 1kHz". Improved exciter (Reaktor): normalised form α/(1 + a·sin(2πft))^2 with DC removed; two Brownian-motion random LFOs at 0.1·f and 0.01·f on the lip parameters; a formant oscillator modulating frequency and a; coloured noise whose amplitude is modulated by the exciter output ("motorboat"). — [Hindle and Posnett 2017](https://softwareprocess.es/pubs/hindle2017NIME-didgeridoo.pdf)
- Code for the above: Pure Data patches (`dijj.pd`, `final_wave.pd`, `simple-tone.pd`…), no README and no licence shown. — [github.com/abramhindle/diggeridoo](https://github.com/abramhindle/diggeridoo)
- Didgitaldoo blog, 2026 [HOBBY]: offline Python "didge2wav": Y(f) = X(f)·Z_bore(f)^1.2·V(f), X = the Hindle exciter (p0 = 100, R = 10, a0 = 1.0, a = 0.85, low-passed by a 2nd-order Butterworth at 2500 Hz), Z_bore from the DidgeLab transmission-line simulation of the bore, V = two Lorentzian peaks at speech F1/F2 (weights 1 and 0.8, floor 0.05, bandwidth 100 Hz) morphing a-e-i-o-u; applied frame by frame in an STFT (Hann 2048, hop 512). — [Didgitaldoo, didge2wav](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/26/didge2wav.html)
- KVR forum thread, 2008 [HOBBY]: proposed chain by "MadBrain": comb / waveguide delay with a filter in the loop; resonant band-pass as the lips; a nonlinearity turning the lip signal into pulses (amplitude-limited or the model "explodes"); two or three parallel band-passes as the vocal tract; multiply by blowing pressure and feed back into the delay; output low-pass and high-pass; reverb. "xoxos" recommended a mass-spring lip with an aperture^2 transmission term and a 5-section Kelly-Lochbaum tract or a few band-passes. No code or parameter values were posted. — [KVR forum](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?t=218612)

### Inferences
- The lip term in eq. 23 is a resistance: dividing through by s_0 gives |U_n| ∝ s_n / |R_lip + Z_V(nω) + Z_D(nω)| with R_lip = (2ρp0)^(1/2)/s_0. With Table I values (p0 = 1 kPa, s_0 = 50 mm^2, ρ = 1.2 kg m^-3) R_lip ≈ 0.98 MPa s m^-3. A purely resistive tract peak of 8 MPa s m^-3 (the measured 1.5 kHz value) would then cut that harmonic by about 19 dB, a 2 MPa peak by about 10 dB, a 10 MPa peak by about 21 dB; at a tract minimum of (L/C)^(1/2)/Q = 0.4 MPa the cut is only about 3 dB. This is the whole "formants are what is left between the notches" mechanism in one line. (My arithmetic from the cited values; the bore term is ignored.)
- Fletcher 1996's eq. 3 with p = R·U is a quadratic in U with the closed form U = (b/2)·[(1 + 4p0/(b·R))^(1/2) - 1], b = γ^2·x^2·R. Its large-b expansion is p0/R - p0^2/(γ^2·R^3·x^2), which is the Hindle "dijj" formula up to the constant γ. The exact form has no singularity as x goes to 0 (U goes to γ·x·p0^(1/2)), so it is the safer one to use as a per-sample "lip flow shaper" (one square root).
- Two descriptions of the tract coexist and are equivalent: 1996 talks about a resonator that enhances flow (formant = resonance), 2006 about impedance maxima that suppress flow (formant = gap between anti-resonances). A synthesizer can implement either peaks or notches; the 2006 picture says the notches are the primary object and their depth scales with tongue height.
- Flared-tube formula, worked example (my arithmetic): for (d2 - d1)/d1 = 0.5 the modes are 1.17, 3.07, 5.04, 7.03 x c/4L' (ratios to the first: 1, 2.61, 4.30, 5.99); for (d2 - d1)/d1 = 1.0 they are 1.31, 3.13, 5.08, 7.06 x c/4L' (ratios 1, 2.39, 3.88, 5.39). The formula is an expansion for small flare and gets less accurate for n = 1 as flare grows.
- Wall-loss check (my arithmetic from eq. 4, d = 40 mm, L = 1.3 m): round-trip amplitude gain exp(-2αL) is 0.85 at 70 Hz, 0.65 at 500 Hz, 0.47 at 1.5 kHz, 0.34 at 3 kHz for β = 12, against 0.97, 0.92, 0.86, 0.81 for a smooth pipe (β = 2.4). Wall loss alone gives Q ≈ 10 for the first resonance at β = 12 and ≈ 51 at β = 2.4, bracketing most of the measured 12 to 86.

No didgeridoo paper by Tamara Smyth was found; her relevant work is on pressure-controlled valves and trombone synthesis (see section 3). The "J. Smith" on the UNSW papers is John Smith of UNSW, not Julius Smith.
- No DAFx, ICMC, SMC or JASA paper presenting a real-time physical-model didgeridoo synthesizer was found; the only NIME paper (Hindle and Posnett) excites a real tube.
- Hollenberg 2000 was available only as an abstract (the CSIRO page redirected to a 404 when fetched directly), so its lip parameters (mass, stiffness, damping, closure law) are not captured here. It is the only source that actually ran a time-domain didgeridoo lip simulation.
- The 2006 theory paper gives no numerical lip mass, stiffness or damping; a physical model would have to borrow them from brass literature (section 3).
- Fletcher 1983 (Australian Aboriginal Studies), Wiggins 1988, Amir 2001 / 2003 / 2004 and Caussé et al. 2004 (IRCAM, eight didgeridoos) are cited by the 2006 papers but were not read.

## 2. Simpler signal models: anti-resonances, bore resonances, waveshaping, heterodyne vocalisation

### Takeaway
The literature supports a cheap three-stage signal model: a lip-area pulse whose harmonics fall as n^-2 and which is closed about half the cycle; a flow divider that puts wide notches at the tract impedance maxima (about 1.5, 2.1, 2.8 kHz for high tongue, 2 to 10 MPa s m^-3 against a lip resistance near 1 MPa s m^-3); and the bore as a comb tuned 2 to 3% below the drone. Vocalisation is a product of two pulse-like valve areas, not of a source and a sine.

### Cited Findings
- The quantity that sets the spectral envelope is the frequency dependence of Z_V at the lips; harmonics near Z_V maxima are suppressed and "formant bands appear near impedance minima". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Per-harmonic flow: |U_n| = 2·p0·s_n / {[(2ρp0)^(1/2) + s_0·R_n]^2 + s_0^2·X_n^2}^(1/2), with R_n + jX_n = Z_V + Z_D at harmonic n. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Measured positions and sizes of the high-tongue tract impedance peaks: about 1.5, 2.1, 2.8 kHz; 2 to 10 MPa s m^-3 (8 MPa s m^-3 at 1.5 kHz in the example); low tongue: peaks "not as large", no envelope shaping. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- The 2006 calculation used tract minima (i.e. formants) at 1.5 and 2.5 kHz with Q = 10 and characteristic impedance 4 MPa s m^-3. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- The formant corresponds approximately to speech F2 for the same mouth shape, not to F1 (F1 depends mainly on mouth opening, and the lips are almost shut in playing); tongue shapes with low impedance peaks "may not produce a clear formant". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Source: lip area open about half the cycle, harmonics s_n ≈ s_0/n^2, steeper above n = 5 for low tongue. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Bore as a filter on the flow: output flow ≈ (Z21/Z22)·input flow, with maxima at the bore's input-impedance maxima, approximately (2n+1)·c/4L for a cylinder; "the resonances of the pipe are closely spaced in frequency compared to those of the tract". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf); [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Odd / even pattern: with the drone 3% above the first resonance, odd harmonics are favoured at low frequency, even ones from about 1 kHz, odd again from about 3.5 kHz (cylinder); on a flared bore there is no such pattern and harmonics 2 to 5 are weak. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Good instruments have low impedance and weak maxima between 1 and 2 kHz, which gives the player's tract control of the formants there. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Radiation: transfer from flow to radiated sound rises at 6 dB/octave and is flat above about 3 kHz. — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Vocalisation, theory: each valve modulates the flow at its own frequency, U = A·<sin ω_D·t - x1>·<sin ω_V·t - x2> (x1, x2 set the closed fraction, negative values dropped), giving components at all m·ω_D ± n·ω_V, strongest for small m and n. Singing a fifth (3:2) or, "more usually", a tenth (5:2) above the drone produces a subharmonic at ω_D/2 with all its harmonics. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Vocalisation, measured: drone f = 70 Hz with voice g = 3f/2 = 105 Hz gives components equally spaced at f/2 = 35 Hz; flow is "proportional to S1·S2" (fold area times lip area), ignoring tract impedance. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- In the 5:2 case there is "not much radiated energy in this sub-octave fundamental, but the subjective pitch is generated strongly from the sequence of harmonics". — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Waveshaping precedent: a 1994/95 Csound instrument by Michel Peters (posted to the Csound list in 1999): sine oscillator (amplitude 0.3, pitch 65 Hz in the test note) into a waveshaping table, plus white noise (amplitude 0.05), through one `reson` at 1000 Hz with bandwidth 1000 Hz ± 500 Hz random (5 Hz), random pitch and amplitude jitter (0.02 at 20 Hz and 10 Hz), `balance` to restore level. No formant motion, no bore. — [Csound mailing list](https://ml.csound.1bpm.net/mailinglist.csound/thread/81560) [HOBBY]
- Formant synthesis in general uses "cascade or parallel second-order filter sections, one per formant"; FOF implements the parallel form in the time domain. — [J. O. Smith, PASP, Formant Synthesis Models](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Formant_Synthesis_Models.html)
- Faust's `physmodels.lib` ships vowel formant tables (five formants; frequency, gain, bandwidth; five voice types x a, e, i, o, u; values from the Csound manual) and FOF / band-pass formant filter banks. — [faustlibraries, physmodels.lib](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/master/physmodels.lib) [CODE]

### Inferences
- Notch filter that follows directly from eq. 23. Model one tract impedance peak as a parallel resonance Z_V(s) = R_p·(s·ω0/Q)/(s^2 + s·ω0/Q + ω0^2) and the lip as a resistance R_lip. The flow gain R_lip/(R_lip + Z_V) is then (s^2 + s·ω0/Q + ω0^2)/(s^2 + s·(ω0/Q)(1 + r) + ω0^2), r = R_p/R_lip. That is exactly a peaking-EQ cut: centre gain -20·log10(1 + r) dB, and in RBJ-cookbook terms Q_eq = Q/(1 + r)^(1/2). So "tongue height" maps to one number r per peak (0 for low tongue, up to about 8 to 10 for high tongue with R_lip ≈ 1 MPa s m^-3), and three peaking biquads at about 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz reproduce the measured structure. Cost: 3 biquads, roughly 15 multiplies per sample; coefficients can be updated at control rate. Because r also depends on R_lip = (2ρp0)^(1/2)/s_0, louder playing with a wider lip opening (larger s_0) deepens the notches and higher pressure makes them shallower; this couples timbre to dynamics for free.
- With r near 8 the notch is wide (denominator Q of about 1 for a tract Q of 10), so the passbands between the notches are narrow: that is why the result is heard as formants near 1.8 kHz and 2.4 kHz rather than as holes. The existing two parallel band-pass "vowel formants" are the complementary approximation; replacing or supplementing them with cascaded cuts gives the right behaviour when the vowel is neutral (flat, not band-limited) and makes the formant depth a single physical control.
- Vowel tables: the yidaki formant tracks speech F2 (roughly 1 to 2.2 kHz), with only weak evidence of a band near 500 Hz. A speech vowel table that drives F1 and F2 equally will overstate F1. A better-founded mapping is: tongue position sets the notch frequencies (and hence the gap near F2), tongue height sets notch depth.
- Bore as a comb. For a lossless cylinder Z21/Z22 = 1/cos(kL) = 2·e^(-jkL)/(1 + e^(-2jkL)): a feedback comb with inverting feedback and loop delay 2L/c (334 samples at 44.1 kHz, 364 at 48 kHz for L = 1.3 m; first peak at c/4L = 66 Hz). Put the loss in the loop as a low-pass whose gain follows exp(-2·10^-5·β·L·ω^(1/2)/d). Tuning the comb 2 to 3% below the oscillator reproduces the measured odd to even to odd migration without any extra logic. For a flared bore, replace the comb by 4 to 6 parallel resonators at the stretched mode frequencies of eq. 2, with gains falling quickly above the second mode for a "good" instrument (Smith et al. 2007). The existing "band-pass at the fundamental" is the first of these resonators.
- Spectral slope budget: lip area -12 dB/octave (n^-2), radiation +6 dB/octave up to about 3 kHz, flat above. Net radiated envelope before tract and bore shaping: about -6 dB/octave up to 3 kHz, then -12 dB/octave. A band-limited sawtooth (-6 dB/octave) followed by a one-pole low-pass near 3 kHz therefore already matches the published first-order envelope; a narrow pulse (flat spectrum) is brighter than anything the measurements show, and the low-tongue case is darker than a sawtooth above the 5th harmonic.
- A better-founded source than saw-to-pulse morphing: generate x(t) = max(0, a0 + a·sin ωt) with a ≥ a0 (closed fraction = arccos(a0/a)/π, about 0.5 when a0 is near 0), then U from the closed-form quadratic above. Parameters are then physical: closed fraction, pressure p0, and load resistance R (which flattens the pulse top). Aliasing has to be handled (oversampling or a band-limited table indexed by closed fraction), since the kinks at closure produce n^-2 harmonics.
- Vocalisation: replace "sine ring-modulating the source" by the product of two unipolar, partly closed pulse waves (eq. 28). With a sine as the voice the output contains only f_V ± n·f_D; with a pulse-like fold area it contains m·f_V ± n·f_D for several m, which is what the measured spectra show, and the 5:2 and 3:2 ratios then give the octave-down "growl" by construction. The product should be taken before the tract notches and the bore comb, since both valves modulate the same flow.
- Breath noise: turbulence is generated in the jet, which exists only while the lips are open; modulating part of the noise by the lip flow (as Hindle and Posnett do, and as aspiration noise is treated in speech synthesis) is better founded than adding stationary pink noise.

### Gaps
- No paper gives Q values or bandwidths for the measured tract impedance peaks; only "broad peaks" and the model's Q = 10 for the resonances. The notch widths above are therefore model-derived, not measured.
- No source gives tract impedance curves as data files, or as a function of a continuous tongue position; only two configurations (high, low) are documented.
- Nothing was found on FM synthesis of the didgeridoo, and nothing on subharmonic generation other than the heterodyne (two-valve) mechanism.
- The first-order formula is stated by its authors to be inaccurate at low frequencies, so levels of harmonics 1 to about 5 should be tuned by ear or against recordings, not derived from it.

## 3. Efficient real-time lip-reed (brass) models and what a didgeridoo needs

### Takeaway
The cheapest established lip-reed model (Cook's, in STK `Brass` and Faust `brassModel`) is a delay line, one biquad "lip" resonator whose squared, clipped output acts as a reflection / transmission gate, and a DC blocker; it costs a few dozen operations per sample. A didgeridoo version needs four changes: lip resonator Q that does not collapse at 60 to 80 Hz, a frequency-dependent loop loss and a flared-bore termination, an upstream (vocal tract) branch sharing the lip flow, and lips that close for half the cycle. Finite-difference bore models are one to two orders of magnitude more expensive and only just real time.

### Cited Findings
- STK `Brass` [CODE] is "a simple brass instrument waveguide model" after Cook's TBone and HosePlayer. Per sample: breathPressure = maxPressure·ADSR + vibrato; mouthPressure = 0.3·breathPressure; borePressure = 0.85·(delay-line output); deltaPressure = mouthPressure - borePressure; deltaPressure = lipFilter(deltaPressure) ("force -> position"); deltaPressure squared ("position -> area"); clamped to at most 1.0; output = deltaPressure·mouthPressure + (1 - deltaPressure)·borePressure; then DC-blocked and written into the delay line. — [STK Brass.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thestk/stk/master/include/Brass.h)
- STK `Brass` constants: lip filter is a biquad resonance at the note frequency with pole radius 0.997 and gain 0.03; delay length = 2·fs/frequency + 3 samples; ADSR times (0.005, 0.001, 1.0, 0.010); vibrato 6.137 Hz; controller 2 "lip tension" sets the lip frequency to target·4^(2·norm - 1), controller 4 "slide length" scales the delay by 0.5 + norm. — [STK Brass.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thestk/stk/master/src/Brass.cpp)
- J. O. Smith's description: in STK the lip valve is "a second-order resonator whose output is squared and hard-clipped"; Cook's earlier one-mass "swinging door" model "works surprisingly well"; lip flow follows Bernoulli, p_m = p_l + ρ·u_l^2/2, and the jet dissipates in the mouthpiece without pressure recovery; cylindrical and conical bores are plain waveguides, flaring sections need two-port filters. — [PASP, Modeling the Lips and Mouthpiece](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Modeling_Lips_Mouthpiece.html)
- Faust `physmodels.lib` [CODE]: `brassModel(tubeLength, lipsTension, mute, pressure)` = chain(brassLips : openTube(maxTubeLength = 5 m, tunedLength) : wBell(mute)). `brassLips` uses bore reflection gain 0.85, pressure scale 0.3, a DC blocker (about 35 Hz), and a lip resonator (TPT second-order section) with input gain 0.012 (comment: STK uses 0.03), bandwidth parameter om = 0.003·(freq/440)·44100/SR, output clipped to ±1; lip frequency = l2f(tubeLength)·4^(2·lipsTension - 1), so tension 0.5 puts the lips on the tube resonance. `wBell` is a one-pole low-pass reflection whose pole is the "mute" value. Documented caveat: the model can be "unstable or sound poor with bad parameters". The MIDI wrapper accepts 50 to 1000 Hz. — [faustlibraries, physmodels.lib](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/master/physmodels.lib)
- Mass-spring lip with Bernoulli flow as used in a finite-difference brass environment [PR]: y'' + σ·y' + (2π·f_lip)^2·y = S_r·Δp/μ, Δp = p_mouth - p(0, t); flow into the bore = w·max(y + H, 0)·sign(Δp)·(2|Δp|/ρ)^(1/2) + S_r·y'. Example trumpet values: S_r = 1.46e-5 m^2, μ = 5.37e-5 kg, σ = 5, H = 0.29 mm, w = 10 mm, f_lip = 550 Hz, mouth pressure ramping to 5 kPa, FS = 44100. — [Harrison et al., Computer Music Journal 2015](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/21585566/Harrison_etal_CMJ_2015_An_Articulated_Brass_Instrument_Synthesis.pdf)
- Cost of the finite-difference approach: 1 s of audio at 44.1 kHz on an Intel Core i5-4300U took 14.02 s (MATLAB), 1.974 s (serial C), 0.857 s (AVX) for a 1.3 m trumpet with one valve; 3.63 s (AVX) for a 4.5 m horn; grid "on the order of a few hundred elements"; the authors say computational cost "is higher than for other methods" such as digital waveguides. — [Harrison et al. 2015](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/21585566/Harrison_etal_CMJ_2015_An_Articulated_Brass_Instrument_Synthesis.pdf)
- Smyth and Scott 2011 (EURASIP J. Adv. Signal Process.) [PR]: trombone synthesis from two transfer functions estimated partly from measurement, one at the mouthpiece (bore feedback, coupled to a lip-valve model) and one outside the bell; aimed at interactive real-time use. A later hobby re-implementation reports hand-designing the bell filters from Scott's thesis and failing to get the mouthpiece model to work. — [Smyth and Scott 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/56375293.pdf); [Scott MSc thesis, SFU](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/12432); [Trombone Simulator](https://nuchi.github.io/trombone) (all three via search-result summaries; the PDFs could not be fetched)
- Smith's review recommends, as the cost-effective brass architecture, a finite-difference lip valve, a digital-waveguide bore and a truncated-IIR bell filter. — [J. O. Smith, Brasses](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/jnmr/Brasses.html) (via search-result summary)
- Cullen, Gilbert and Campbell 2000 (Acustica 86, 704-724) [PR]: linear stability analysis of threshold pressure and threshold frequency against lip and air-column parameters, checked on a trombone with an artificial mouth; the artificial lips show several mechanical resonances, each classifiable as inward- or outward-striking. — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00474993) (abstract level)
- Didgeridoo-specific requirements stated in the literature: drone 55 to 80 Hz; played 2 to 3% above the first bore resonance with an outward-swinging (+,-) valve; second mode at about 2.5 to 3 times the drone; lips closed about half the cycle; tract and bore impedances of comparable size between 1 and 3 kHz (unlike other lip-valve instruments, where the bore maxima are "very much larger"); no mouthpiece cup, which in brass acts as a roughly 500 Hz Helmholtz resonator that also filters out mouth-resonance effects. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf); [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)

### Inferences
- Per-sample cost of the STK / Faust structure: one biquad, one interpolated delay read and write, one one-pole DC blocker, a square, a clamp and three multiply-adds; well under the cost of the current three filter paths. Memory: at most about 1300 samples of delay for a 70 Hz note in STK's two-period layout, about 335 for a physical 1.3 m closed-open tube.
- Lip resonator Q at low pitch. STK's fixed pole radius 0.997 is a bandwidth of about 42 Hz at 44.1 kHz: Q ≈ 5 at 220 Hz but only ≈ 1.7 at 70 Hz. Faust scales the bandwidth with frequency (om ∝ freq), which works out to a constant Q of about 10. A didgeridoo port should set the radius from a target Q, r = exp(-π·f_lip/(Q·fs)) (0.9990 for Q = 5 at 70 Hz and 44.1 kHz), and use a state-variable / TPT form or double precision, since direct-form biquads lose accuracy with poles that close to z = 1.
- Bore. Replace the flat 0.85 loop gain by the wall-loss low-pass computed in section 1 (0.85 at 70 Hz happens to equal Fletcher's β = 12 value, but the real loss rises to about 0.47 at 1.5 kHz), add an open-end reflection filter, and for a flared instrument either a conical waveguide section or a short all-pass / dispersion filter that stretches the modes to f2/f1 ≈ 2.6 to 3.0. The overblown "toot" then appears by raising lip frequency and pressure (4 to 5 kPa against 1 to 2 kPa) instead of by a programmed pitch jump.
- Upstream tract. The physics puts Z_V and Z_D in series for the same flow U. In wave variables: p_D = 2·p_D^- + Z0_D·U and p_M = p_0 + 2·p_M^- - Z0_V·U (flow leaves the mouth), so Δp = p_0 + 2(p_M^- - p_D^-) - (Z0_V + Z0_D)·U. Combined with U = S·(2Δp/ρ)^(1/2) this is a quadratic in U with a closed-form root, so no iteration is needed per sample. The upstream side can be a second short waveguide (a 17 cm tract is about 22 samples one way at 44.1 kHz) with a nearly closed glottis reflection, or two to three lumped resonators. Characteristic impedances: bore 0.2 to 1 MPa s m^-3 (0.33 for 40 mm); tract 1.3 MPa s m^-3 for a 20 mm duct and 5.2 MPa s m^-3 for a 10 mm constriction (Fletcher uses 4). The STK-style reflection gate has no place for an upstream load, so adding the tract means moving to an explicit flow (Bernoulli) junction of this kind.
- Lip closure. The measured half-cycle closure with soft contact means the lip model needs a collision term (a one-sided extra stiffness and damping when y + H < 0), not just the clamp on the area. Hollenberg's abstract says the closure damping strongly controls harmonic generation, so this parameter is the brightness control of a physical model.
- Lip parameters must be rescaled from brass values: the trumpet example has f_lip = 550 Hz with μ = 5.37e-5 kg; a 70 Hz valve with the same stiffness needs about (550/70)^2 ≈ 62 times the mass, or far lower stiffness. Only s_0 = 50 mm^2 and p_0 = 1 kPa are given for the didgeridoo.
- Risk assessment: the simplified models are documented as fragile ("unstable or sound poor with bad parameters"), a self-oscillating model has a start-up transient and a threshold pressure, and its pitch is an outcome (lip resonance pulled by the bore), not an input. A monophonic drone instrument tolerates that better than a keyboard instrument, but exact MIDI pitch needs a tuning correction table. A hybrid is lower-risk: keep the oscillator-driven source (pitch exact), and take from the physical model only the closed-form flow junction, the tract notches and the bore comb.

### Gaps
- No lip mass, stiffness, damping or collision parameters for the didgeridoo are published in the sources read; Hollenberg 2000 (full text not obtained) is the likely source.
- The Smyth and Scott paper, Scott's thesis and Cullen's paper were not readable in full, so their generalised valve equations and lip parameter values are not quoted here.
- No source gives measured CPU load for a waveguide brass model; the cost statements above are operation counts from the code.
- No published waveguide model includes a player's vocal tract upstream of a lip valve with numbers for the didgeridoo; the flow-junction formulation above is standard practice (McIntyre-Schumacher-Woodhouse style, cited by Harrison et al.) applied by me, not a published didgeridoo design.

## 4. Existing instruments that imitate the didgeridoo

### Takeaway
The commercial field is sample libraries; none of the vendor pages found claims a physical model, and the only product that exposes vowel control as a continuous parameter (FluffyAudio DidgHybridoo) does it by morphing sampled vowels through a formant filter. Synthesis attempts are hobby-level (waveshaping, synth plus wah plus short delay, the Fletcher exciter), and their authors mostly report that the result sounds robotic or unlike the instrument.

### Inferences
- What the products treat as essential performance controls, in order of how often they appear: tempo-locked rhythm (sampled grooves, arpeggiators, rhythmic modulators), vowel / formant movement, growl or vocalisation, beatbox-style staccato attacks. Toots and breath are rarely exposed separately. A synthesizer that offers continuous vowel, voice and toot control under one rhythm engine is doing something the sample libraries largely do not.
- The recurring complaint about synthetic versions is not spectral accuracy but stasis: Hindle and Posnett's "robotic", the SOS verdict, and the need for sample-and-hold or random modulation in the forum reports all point to slow random variation of pitch, lip opening and formant position as a necessity. Brownian (random-walk) modulators at about 0.1·f0 and 0.01·f0 are the one concrete published recipe.
- DidgeLab or didgmo can turn a bore geometry into a list of resonance frequencies and magnitudes, which is exactly the parameter set a resonator-bank bore model needs; this gives "instrument presets" (cylindrical PVC, Eastern Arnhem near-cylinder, Western Arnhem flare) a defensible basis. DidgeLab's non-commercial licence matters if its code, rather than its output numbers, were to be reused.

### Gaps
- No commercial electronic didgeridoo (a dedicated hardware instrument) was found.
- No Yamaha VL1 / VL70-m didgeridoo preset could be confirmed, nor a didgeridoo preset in any current physical-modelling plug-in; searches returned only general pages. This is absence of evidence from two searches, not proof.
- No Sound On Sound or comparable professional review of a didgeridoo sample library was found; opinions on realism come from one KVR user review and vendor copy. No player-forum comparison of libraries was found.
- 8dio and Sample Logic (Bohemian) didgeridoo content surfaced in search results but was not examined. No SuperCollider or Faust didgeridoo patch was found.
- How DidgHybridoo's formant engine works internally is not documented.

Recordings were made at 44.1 kHz; impedance at 5.383 Hz spacing from 0.2 to 3.0 kHz. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Computed target: radiated spectrum of the Table I model, odd and even harmonics marked separately (Fig. 3b). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Circular breathing timing in the measured example: inhalation phases of typically 0.2 s, amplitude falling during each inhalation; rhythm of exhalations each followed by a brief inhalation. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Instrument population statistics (38 traditional instruments): f1 about 50 to 105 Hz; f2/f1 about 2.6 to 3.0; Q1 12 to 86; Z0 about 0.2 to 1 MPa s m^-3; Z1 about 5 to 40 MPa s m^-3; pitch on average 2% above f1; impedance spectra of the best, a middle and the worst instrument (Fig. 2). Sound examples of instruments of each quality class played by one musician are referenced at the Didjshop site. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Three Arnhem Land instruments with dimensions and drone frequencies 60, 80 and 64 Hz, and two formant spectra (about 1.5 kHz and about 2.2 kHz). — [Fletcher 1996](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)
- Large unanalysed audio collections: AudioSet has a "Didgeridoo" class (about 1,240 YouTube clips per the search summary) and MusicCaps has captioned didgeridoo clips; neither carries spectral or formant annotation. — [AudioSet ontology, Didgeridoo](https://research.google.com/audioset/ontology/didgeridoo.html) (via search-result summary)

### Inferences
- A practical validation set for the synthesizer can be built from the three UNSW WAVs: (a) the high / low tongue pair fixes notch depth and the formant position between 1.5 and 2 kHz; (b) the circular-breathing file fixes the amplitude dip and timbre change of the cheek-reservoir phase (about 0.2 s); (c) the vocalisation file fixes the heterodyne spacing (35 Hz for a 70 Hz drone with a 105 Hz voice). All three were played on a 1.2 m cylindrical pipe, so they validate the cylinder case, not a flared traditional instrument.
- The 2007 ranges are directly usable as parameter limits: drone 50 to 105 Hz, overblow ratio 2.6 to 3.0 (about 16.5 to 19 semitones), first-resonance Q 12 to 86.
- Licensing of the UNSW files is not stated on the pages read; they should be treated as reference material for analysis, not as content to ship.

### Gaps
- No open dataset with formant tracks, harmonic amplitudes or tract impedance as numbers was found (Zenodo, Freesound and general searches); figures would have to be digitised or the WAVs re-analysed.
- No published analysis of traditional Arnhem Land performance recordings with model parameters (formant trajectories over a rhythm cycle, toot timing, pressure curves) was found within this scope; the measurements all come from laboratory playing on pipes.
- The Didjshop sound examples referenced by Smith et al. 2007 were not checked for availability.
