## 1. The vocabulary of sounds a player uses

### Takeaway
In the best-documented traditional style (Yolŋu "hard tongue", *ŋäṉarr-ḏäl*) the vocabulary is small and percussive: a drone cut into pulses by two named tongue motions (*ḏith-dhu*, *dhirrḻ*), each fired together with a single push of air from the belly, plus quick stabs of the first overblown note (*ḏup*) that fall straight back to the drone (*pu*), and a subtle voiced growl. Vowel sweeps, "hahaha" belly-pulse rhythms, double/triple tonguing, wobble, drop-jaw pitch bends, animal-call vocals and beatbox pops belong to contemporary, non-traditional playing.

### Cited Findings

**The plain drone**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` The drone is the fundamental, made by buzzing "fairly relaxed" lips sealed inside the mouthpiece. Many (non-Yolŋu) players play only this note and vary it with mouth shape and tongue, written "weeeooooaaaaaow". — [Yidaki Story: The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Yolŋu shape the mouth only subtly; they do not play slow sounds or "swirling high harmonics": "It is all about the rhythm." — [Yidaki Story: The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)
- `[ACOUST]` Moving tongue and mouth shifts strong formants that the ear tracks closely, especially when they move; e.g. tongue near the roof of the mouth leaves a band near 1.8 kHz that sounds somewhat like "ee". — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)

**Tongue articulations and their spoken mnemonics (Yolŋu)**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Two tongue positions are used: *retroflexed* (tongue curled back, underside of the tip on the gum ridge) and *interdental* (tongue thrust forward, tip between the teeth). — [Yidaki Story: Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` *ḏith-dhu* (often just "dith" on Djalu' Gurruwiwi's teaching CDs): the tongue uncurls from retroflexed to interdental together with a pulse of air from the belly; with the right lip/mouth/throat pressure this gives "a brief hint of the trumpet note"; on "dhu" the tongue pulls back to neutral. — [Yidaki Story: Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` *dhirrḻ* (or the closely related *dhirrk*): the reverse motion, interdental back to retroflexed, flicking the gum ridge on the way (the "rr"); it comes with a strong air pulse and often a breath in. — [Yidaki Story: Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Further mnemonic spellings on Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr's CD *Hard Tongue Didgeridoo*: *ḏith-dhu*, *dhirrḻ-ḻo*, *ḏup-pu*, *dhirrḻ*; exercises are given in "slow" and "fast" versions (Exercise 2 *ḏith-dhu* fast; Exercise 4 *dhirrḻ-ḻo* slow; Exercise 9 *ḏup-pu dhirrḻ dhirrḻ* fast). — [Yidaki Story: Hard Tongue Didgeridoo](https://yidakistory.com/?p=95)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Non-Yolŋu "flashy" rhythms use brass-style double or triple tonguing, written "taka taka" / "takata takata"; Yolŋu playing does not, relying on tongue motions from Yolŋu languages. — [Yidaki Story: Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[COLLECTOR]` "Traditional Aboriginal techniques are based on pulsed accents effected through tongue, throat and diaphragm manipulations." — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)

**Belly / diaphragm pushes (accents)**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` A pulse of air from the stomach is "like saying 'ha'". Many players worldwide make rhythms from quick "hahahaha" belly effects. Yolŋu use only a *subtle* version to colour slower notes or the introductory drone of a song (audible as "the subtle flutter in some of the long tones" in Djalu' Gurruwiwi's playing). — [Yidaki Story: Pulses of Air](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/pulses-of-air)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` In rhythms, Yolŋu use only *single* pulses, simultaneous with the tongue movements, "for aggressive accents". The strongest is the "dith" of *ḏith-dhu* ("the surge of power"); the same accent also accompanies *dhirrḻ* and trumpeted notes. — [Yidaki Story: Pulses of Air](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/pulses-of-air)

**Throat**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Throat muscles increase the air flow of the tongue motions and deepen the sound; they "provide extra pressure and a sort of extra 'wobble' to the sound", complementing the *dhirrḻ* motion. Yolŋu do not teach it explicitly but "hear it and know it is part of the desired sound". — [Yidaki Story: Throat Muscles](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/throat-muscles)

**Toot / trumpet / horn note**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Made with tighter lips (they move into a "p" shape) while the tongue pushes air and a pulse comes from the lungs. Many yiḏaki can sound a bugle-like series of higher notes, but Yolŋu use only the first; playing higher ones "may result in the giggling of Yolŋu children". — [Yidaki Story: The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` It appears in almost all Yolŋu songs, "often alternating quickly with the lower drone, to cue changes or endings". The quick horn tone is *ḏup* (like "doop"); the return to the drone is *pu*, giving *ḏup-pu*; example pattern "*ḏup-pu dhirrḻ dhirrḻ*", repeated. — [Yidaki Story: The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Isolated, *sustained* trumpeted notes also exist: mid-song, or at the start of some songs as a call to ceremony / a signal to the dancers that the song is about to start. — [Yidaki Story: The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)
- `[ETHNO]` (Moyle-derived summary) In eastern Arnhem Land the overblown tone lies "a seventh to an eleventh above the fundamental, though a tenth seems to be more usual"; it alternates with the fundamental and is used structurally, depending on the song item and the player's expertise. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)
- `[ENCYC]` The second resonance (the overblown note) is "usually around an 11th" above the fundamental (ratio 8:3). — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo); the two sources differ on the usual interval (tenth vs. eleventh).
- `[ACOUST]` (snippet) Brief soundings of the second resonance, roughly a ninth to an eleventh above the drone, are used to interrupt the low note and create a further rhythm. — [Wolfe & Smith, didjeridu review (PDF)](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- `[COLLECTOR]` "Only the first overtone note is used in traditional Aboriginal society." — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)
- `[COLLECTOR]` Catalogued north-east Arnhem instruments: 120–145 cm, fundamentals mainly D to F#, overtones such as G, G#, F#. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)

**Voice: growl, sung notes, animal calls**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Yolŋu do not sing harmonious melodies over the drone; they add "a growly quality to the tone with subtle use of voice along with the rhythm", compared to grunting along with the *dhirrḻ*. — [Yidaki Story: Adding Voice](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/adding-voice)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Yolŋu do not imitate animals on the yiḏaki "for fun or education"; animal sounds are parts of particular traditional songs and imitate the animals' movement in the rhythm (example: Larry Winiwini Gurruwiwi's accompaniment carries the brolga's voice and the rhythm of its dance). — [Yidaki Story: Adding Voice](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/adding-voice)
- `[ACOUST]` Vocalising is "much more common in modern styles than in traditional performance". Singing a fifth above the drone produces a difference tone one octave below the drone; a major third above produces one two octaves below; this very low tone "can be quite loud and impressive". — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- `[ENCYC]` Players add animal-like sounds, mostly dingo or kookaburra, with the vocal folds while blowing; results range "from very high-pitched sounds to much lower sounds" caused by interference between lip and vocal-fold vibration. — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- `[TEACHER]` Lesson sequence for vocals: hum gently while the lips vibrate, keep the airflow steady rather than blowing harder; higher-pitched voice is easier to hear. Taught sounds: dingo howl ("Ooooowwwww" over a steady drone), dog bark, kookaburra ("laughing" call), cockatoo. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: Vocals & sounds](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=306)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Beginner kookaburra is the syllable pattern "koo koo koo kaa kaa kaa"; the advanced version adds a gargling vocal and double tonguing. A dingo-howl exercise is "how many howls you can fit into one breath". — [Didgeridoo Dojo: Kookaburra](https://dev.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=13074), [Didgeridoo Dojo: dingo howl](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=337)

**Contemporary extras: wobble, drop jaw, stops, pops, beatbox**
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Advanced non-traditional techniques taught: a tongue technique that briefly *stops* the drone "for punchy fast rhythms"; a "punchy vocal pop" taken together with a breath; "drop jaw" to lower the drone's pitch; the "wobble" (each player has a different interpretation); overlapping harmonics for an evolving rhythm. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: advanced lessons](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/advanced-didgeridoo-lessons-techniques)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Didgeridoo beatboxing is taught as two separate arts (beatboxing, didgeridoo) merged into one sound, built up from basic beatbox sounds timed into rhythms. — [Didge Project: beatbox tutorial](https://didgeproject.com/beatbox/didgeridoo-beatbox-tutorial-3-essential-rhythms/)
- `[PRESS]` A player's own description of tone shaping: move the cheeks "as though you have a mouthful of mouthwash which you're sloshing from side to side"; mouth movements give a filter sweep; players do animal and bird impressions and even speak words while playing. — [Sound On Sound: make your own didgeridoo](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)

**Percussion on the tube**
- `[ETHNO]` In eastern Arnhem Land the (B-type) player may strike the tube with a stick or flick it with a finger in time with the singer's beats; seated players rest the far end on the ground or in a box, bucket or (traditionally) a bailer shell for resonance. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)

### Inferences
- A Yolŋu accent is a compound event, not a volume change: loudness surge (belly) + a fast tongue transit between two extreme tongue positions (a fast mouth-shape/formant gesture) + sometimes a momentary flash of the overblown note (on "dith"). A step that only ducks the level and nudges the vowel has the wrong sign (duck instead of surge) and lacks the two distinct, opposite tongue gestures.
- The two motions are mirror images (back-to-front for *ḏith*, front-to-back with a tap for *dhirrḻ*), so a pattern needs at least two different step "colours", not one repeated vowel push.
- The traditional toot is mostly a very short stab that returns to the drone inside the same rhythmic cell (*ḏup-pu*); a sustained toot is the special case (song introduction, isolated call). An overblow that lasts as long as the key is held matches only the special case.
- Converting the cited intervals: seventh = 10–11 semitones, ninth = 13–14, tenth = 15–16, eleventh = 17 (8:3 = 16.98 semitones). The interval is a fixed property of each instrument, not chosen by the player.
- The traditional "growl" is rhythmic and subtle (a grunt on the *dhirrḻ*), not a continuously held sung note; held sung intervals (fifth, major third) producing sub-octave tones are the contemporary idiom.

### Gaps
- The mnemonics "ritoru", "wok", "did-n-moro" suggested in the brief were not found in any source I could open; I can confirm only *ḏith-dhu*, *dhirrḻ* / *dhirrk*, *dhirrḻ-ḻo*, *ḏup*, *ḏup-pu*, *pu*. No west Arnhem (mako) mouth-sound syllables were found.
- Alice Moyle's booklet *Aboriginal Sound Instruments* (AIATSIS PDF) returned HTTP 403, and Knopoff's chapter "Accompanying the dreaming" (in Neuenfeldt, *The Didjeridu: From Arnhem Land to Internet*, 1997, pp. 39–67) is only available as a [bibliographic record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/acde1b09-34e3-4447-8ab0-ac11b67f362c); neither could be read.
- No source gave durations in milliseconds for a toot stab, a bark or a tongue stroke.
- Glottal stops, "pops" and pitch drops at phrase ends: only lesson titles/snippets found (drone-stopping tongue technique, vocal pop with breath, drop jaw); no description precise enough to imitate. The Didgeridoo Dojo "Loud Bark" lesson page returned HTTP 500.
- The audio examples on yidakistory.com failed to load in the fetch, so none of the sound descriptions above were checked by listening.

## 2. Rhythm: tempos, cycle lengths, how breathing is woven in, how accent and timbre define the pattern

### Takeaway
Rhythm on the didgeridoo is a pattern of accents and timbre changes on one pitch, and the inhalations are part of the pattern, not gaps in it: in Yolŋu playing a small breath is taken on nearly every *dhirrḻ*. Measured tempos exist for didjeridu-accompanied song in the Daly region and west Arnhem Land (clapstick beat roughly 46–72 bpm slow, 96–120 moderate, 120–146 fast, with doubled beating above that); I found no measured tempos for Yolŋu playing.

### Cited Findings

**Breathing as rhythm**
- `[ACOUST]` During the quick nasal inhalation the sound continues but "its timbre changes sharply", because only the mouth's resonances are involved; players use this timbre shift and changes in loudness to mark the rhythm, so the rhythm comes from the breathing cycle. — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- `[PRESS]` "The tone changes when you switch from releasing air to drawing it in", so breathing should follow the rhythm. — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Non-Yolŋu players use the cheeks as a bellows and can "play for a while, take a big breath, and then play for a while again". Yolŋu keep the cheeks in, avoid long cheek breaths, and take "lots of small breaths supported with and initiated by bursts of air from the belly" (what western players call bounce breathing). — [Yidaki Story: Breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Placement: "Push air out with the belly right as you begin to say 'dhirrl,' and breathe back in through the nose as the tongue motion is completing." In "*ḏith-dhu dhirrḻ dhirrḻ*" the belly pulses at the start of each of the three fragments and the player breathes in on the *dhirrḻ* motions. — [Yidaki Story: Breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Asked how Yolŋu children learn circular breathing, Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr answered "learn what?"; the breathing is a by-product of playing the tongue techniques. — [Yidaki Story: Breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)
- `[TEACHER]` Bounce breathing is "Out-In-Out-In…" at a quick pace, cheeks appearing full the whole time, driven by the abdominals; done too slowly "it doesnt keep the pressure up and keep the sound going". — [Didge Project: bounce breathing](https://www.didgeproject.com/circular-breathing/circular-breathing-basics-didgeridoo-bounce-breathing/)

**Described / notated patterns**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Base teaching rhythm (three-pulse cell, six cells to the line): "dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl". Variation 1: "dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl dith-dhu dhirrl dith-dhu / dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl". Variation 2 (with toots): "dup-pu dhirrl dhirrl dup-pu dhirrl dup-pu / dup-pu dhirrl dhirrl dup-pu dhirrl dhirrl". — [Yidaki Story: Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Beginner patterns, "(B)" = a circular breath: "dar (B) dee (B)…", "wee or (B) wee or (B)…", each to be held for one minute; a wobble pattern "do (B) de do (B) de do (B) de do (B) de do". No bpm is given; the advice is to start slowly and speed up. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: Beginner Rhythm 3](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=377), [Beginner Rhythm 2](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=376), [The wobble #3](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/advanced-didgeridoo-lessons-techniques/wobble3)
- `[COLLECTOR]` North-east Arnhem Land style: "Sharp tongued accents, syncopated overtone rhythms, and variable time metering". Djinang/Djinba (north-central): repeating accented doublets interrupted by "metronomic overtone spurts". Groote Eylandt: tongued doublet and triplet accents as the baseline, overtone used in fast percussive effects. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` (snippet) Yolŋu songs have many changes rather than one repeating rhythm. — [Yidaki Story: When, Where and by Who](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/yolngu-rom/when-where-and-by-who)

**Measured tempos (didjeridu-accompanied song, clapstick beat)**
- `[ETHNO]` *Wangga* (Daly region), clapstick tempo by repertory, in bpm: Barrtjap slow 58–65, moderate 117–120, fast 126–144; Muluk 50–55 / 110–113 / 126–140; Mandji 46–48 / 110–116 / 130–140; Lambudju slow 64–69, slow moderate 99–107, moderate 110–116, fast 120–125; Walakandha (early) 60–72 / 112 / 120–136; Walakandha (post 1986) slow 55–65, fast 133–142; Ma-yawa slow moderate 117–125, moderate 134–146. A "fast doubled" final section is given at about 130 bpm. — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, wangga conventions (Sydney University Press)](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)
- `[ETHNO]` West Arnhem *manyardi* tempo bands: slow about 50–80 bpm, slow moderate 81–95, moderate 96–120, fast / fast doubled 121–300+; examples: Inyjalarrku moderate "around 95–98 bpm", Yanajanak slow moderate 82–93 bpm. — [ANU Press chapter on a west Arnhem Land Mamurrng ceremony](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) In Marri Ngarr church *lirrga* the clapstick tempos fall into four discrete bands, with no songs in the range MM 134–254. — [Barwick, Marri Ngarr lirrga (PDF)](https://core.ac.uk/download/41240478.pdf)

**Rhythmic modes and what the didjeridu does in them**
- `[ETHNO]` Rhythmic modes are defined by clapstick tempo plus beating pattern: even; uneven triple, quadruple, quintuple, sextuple (beats then a rest, e.g. three beats and a rest in "fast uneven quadruple"); doubled (twice the rate of even beating); suspended (sticks stop, pulse carried on). — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, rhythmic modes](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-characteristics-of-the-rhythmic-modes.html)
- `[ETHNO]` In unmeasured mode 1 (no sticks) "the rhythm of the didjeridu is similarly irregular"; the absence of a regular didjeridu pulse is one of the mode's strongest features. In slow mode with suspended sticks "the didjeridu pulse continues to clearly articulate a regular duple subdivision of the beat"; in suspended fast mode the pulse is "maintained by the didjeridu". — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, rhythmic modes](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-characteristics-of-the-rhythmic-modes.html)
- `[ETHNO]` In metrically free sections the didjeridu "tends to mark word and morpheme boundaries with pulsations"; it has "specific patterns associated with each mode" and in measured modes "plays almost as great a role in supporting the vocal line and defining metre" as the sticks. Before a song the singer may sing the required pattern to the player in "didjeridu mouth sounds" (Alice Moyle's term). — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)
- `[ETHNO]` In *manyardi* a change of didjeridu pattern can mirror the clapstick rhythm and cue the sticks to change to fast even beating. — [ANU Press chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)

**Lengths**
- `[ETHNO]` *Manyardi* songs are typically "between one and two minutes in duration". — [ANU Press chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) Yolŋu *manikay* is performed as long series of short song items grouped by subject and, within subjects, by rhythmic mode. — [Cultural Survival: Yolngu popular song](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/nurturing-sacred-through-yolngu-popular-song)

### Inferences
- Breath rate: in the Yolŋu teaching cell two of every three pulses carry an inhalation, so the "inhale timbre" recurs at the pulse rate, alternating with the accent. It is a per-step colour, not a once-per-bar event.
- Pulse rate implied by the Daly figures: a duple subdivision of a 46–72 bpm slow beat is 92–144 didjeridu pulses per minute; fast even beating is 120–146 per minute and doubled beating about twice that (roughly 240–290 stick strokes per minute, consistent with the "121–300+" band and with Barwick's gap of MM 134–254 between fast and doubled).
- Cycle length: the documented Yolŋu teaching patterns are built from 3-pulse cells paired into 6, with the variation placed in the last cell of a line and a two-line (12-cell) period; clapstick cycles in *wangga* come in 3, 4, 5 and 6 beats with one rest. Patterns are therefore short, asymmetric and vary at the line end; they are not an even stream of identical 1/8 or 1/16 steps.
- *ḏith-dhu* and *ḏup-pu* are two-part cells (accent then release) while *dhirrḻ* is one gesture, so cells are unequal inside even when they occupy equal time.
- Contemporary bounce breathing has a lower speed limit (it fails when done too slowly), which suggests a floor on plausible pulse rates for that style; no number was given.

### Gaps
- No tempo in bpm was found for Yolŋu *manikay* / *buŋgul* accompaniment or for Milkayŋu's "slow"/"fast" exercises.
- No notated (staff or grid) transcription of a didjeridu part could be read; the mode-specific didjeridu patterns in the *wangga* and *manyardi* sources are images or not described.
- No source stated the interval between breaths in seconds for the cheek-bellows (contemporary) style.

## 3. Regional traditional styles and contemporary styles; reference players and recordings

### Takeaway
The standard division is overtone-present (east: Yolŋu, Groote Eylandt, north-central Arnhem Land) versus overtone-absent (west Arnhem Land, north-west NT, Kimberley). The eastern style is dense, hard-tongued and punctuated by toots; the western is a continuous drone with slight pitch inflection and voiced colouring. Everywhere the instrument traditionally accompanies a singer with clapsticks; solo, slow, "swirling" and vocal-effect playing is contemporary.

### Cited Findings

**The two accompaniment types**
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) "A-type" = overtone-absent style of west Arnhem Land; "B-type" = overtone-present style of east Arnhem Land. — [Duke University Musical Instrument Collections](https://sites.duke.edu/dumic/instruments/woodwinds/oceania/didgeridoo/)
- `[ETHNO]` A-type: the fundamental gives a continuous drone with slight pitch inflections or colouring from a vocalised sound; found among "pre-fixing" language groups (Goulburn and Croker Islands, mainland west of the Liverpool River, coast north and south of Darwin, the Kimberley, and from Oenpelli through Bamyili to the Gulf). B-type: Yolŋu ("Yuulngu") speaking groups east of the Liverpool River to the east coast, including Groote Eylandt. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)
- `[ETHNO]` Posture differs: A-type players hold the far end above the ground (moving it side to side or resting it on a foot); B-type players rest it on the ground or in a resonator. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)

**North-east Arnhem Land (Yolŋu, yiḏaki)**
- `[COLLECTOR]` "Sharp tongued accents, syncopated overtone rhythms, and variable time metering." — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` The *ŋäṉarr-ḏäl* ("hard tongue") style "has been called the hardest didge playing to learn". — [Yidaki Story: Hard Tongue Didgeridoo](https://yidakistory.com/?p=95)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Almost all Yolŋu play straight on (not from the side of the mouth) because the style relies on the tongue pushing air straight out. Performances are accompaniment to song and dance, not solos. — [Yidaki Story: The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` (snippet) Mainly young men play in ceremony; some sacred yiḏaki parts are used only in closed ceremony. — [Yidaki Story: When, Where and by Who](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/yolngu-rom/when-where-and-by-who)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Clans prefer different sounds: Djalu' Gurruwiwi (Gälpu) prefers deep, powerful instruments (his large E yiḏaki: 157 cm, "incredibly strong and warm bass response"); a Wangurri F# instrument (136 cm) is described as "dirtier", with more midrange and a growling tone. The rule "Yirritja = short and high, Dhuwa = long and low" is called "a questionable generalization". — [Yidaki Story: Dhuwa and Yirritja yiḏaki](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=312)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) *Manikay*: a small group of male singers in heterophony, accompanying themselves on *bilma* (paired sticks), with one yiḏaki player and dancers. — [Cultural Survival](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/nurturing-sacred-through-yolngu-popular-song)
- `[ETHNO]` An ensemble may have two or more stick-beating singers but never more than one didjeridu. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)

**North-central Arnhem Land and Groote Eylandt**
- `[COLLECTOR]` North-central (Djinang, Djinba, Gupapuyŋu): overtone-present but "slower and deliberately sustained", unlike the dense, rapid north-east style. Groote Eylandt (*yiraga*): overtone in fast percussive effects over tongued doublets and triplets. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)

**West Arnhem Land (mako / mago, arrawirr)**
- `[ENCYC]` The mako is usually shorter, with "a slightly fuller and richer sound, but without the 'overtone' note"; the name was popularised by David Blanasi, a Bininj man whose language was Kunwinjku. — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- `[COLLECTOR]` Mago are "somewhat richer and more full-bodied"; overtone playing is not a feature and most mago do not sound the overtone easily; catalogued examples 105–132 cm in E, E flat, F, G. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)
- `[ENCYC]` (snippet) *Kun-borrk* songs nearly always start with the didjeridu, then sticks and voice (at Gunbalanya: didjeridu, voice, then sticks); they most commonly end with the didjeridu stopping first; David Blanasi is named as a master of kun-borrk, continued by his grandson Darryl Dikarrna. — [Wikipedia: Indigenous music of Australia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunggul)
- `[ETHNO]` In *manyardi* (Goulburn Islands) the didjeridu starts before the singers' first text phrase; the singers vocalise on the didjeridu's "tonic" pitch in instrumental sections; songs end with voice and didjeridu finishing first, "leaving the clapsticks to beat a final 'terminating pattern'". — [ANU Press chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)

**Daly region, north-west NT, Kimberley (wangga, lirrga; overtone-absent)**
- `[COLLECTOR]` North-west NT (Port Keats, Daly River): overtone-absent, related to west Arnhem Land but "less developed"; Kimberley *wangga* accompaniment: "simple and repetitive", no overtone. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)
- `[ETHNO]` In *wangga* the lowest note of most melodic sections is the didjeridu's pitch (one example is in E flat); the didjeridu "always continues throughout the vocal section" and plays the instrumental sections with the sticks. — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)
- `[ENCYC]` In *wangga* the song-man starts with voice and then brings in *bilma* with didgeridoo accompaniment; the didgeridoo and clapstick rhythms "are precise" and handed down over generations. — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)

**Contemporary / "world music" styles**
- `[COLLECTOR]` Contemporary playing is "swirling, bouncing sounds with plenty of vocal effects"; the more accomplished contemporary players are "driven by beat and rhythm"; some Aboriginal players compare contemporary playing to aeroplane noises. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)
- `[ENCYC]` Slide didgeridoo: Charlie McMahon's *didjeribone*, two sliding plastic tubes, played "somewhat in the manner of a trombone". Continuous playing of over 40 minutes is recorded; Mark Atkins plays over 50 minutes continuously on *Didgeridoo Concerto* (1994). — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- `[TEACHER]` Players held up by a teaching site: Ondrej Smeykal (Prague; "a one-man didgeridoo orchestra", from "break-beat and organic Didgeridoo techno, to trance, ambient"); Zalem Delarbre (France; fast complex rhythms, beatboxing, wobble-based beginnings, his own "Didgstep"); William Thoren (multi-drone instrument and technique); Shibaten (Japan; "deep meditative sound baths" to dance music); Ganga Giri, David Hudson, Mark Atkins, Xavier Rudd, Stephen Kent. — [Didge Project: players you should know](https://www.didgeproject.com/artists/didgeridoo-players-you-should-know/)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Dubravko Lapaine is described as playing "multiple melodies and rhythms" simultaneously and working on extra-long instruments. — [profile](https://and.nmartproject.net/?p=1588)
- `[ACOUST]` William Barton is cited as a virtuoso soloist producing a wide range of sounds, with orchestras. — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)

**Reference players and recordings named by sources**
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` *Hard Tongue Didgeridoo* by Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr (Buku-Larrŋgay Mulka, Yirrkala; out of print on CD, digital available) teaches the style from the basics with spoken mouth sounds; Djalu' Gurruwiwi's instructional CDs go further. Players to watch: Djalu' Gurruwiwi, Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr, Mirarra Burarrwaŋa, Djakapurra Munyarryun, Mirrwatŋa Munyarryun, Larry Winiwini Gurruwiwi, Mizak Babamiku Gurruwiwi. The page adds that it may not be possible to learn Yolŋu playing without learning from Yolŋu in person. — [Yidaki Story: Conclusion](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/conclusion/), [Hard Tongue Didgeridoo](https://yidakistory.com/?p=95)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) Alice Moyle's field CD *Aboriginal Music from North-Eastern Arnhem Land including Groote Eylandt* (recorded 1962–63). — [AIATSIS](https://aiatsis.gov.au/publication/35854)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) A. P. Elkin's 1949–52 recordings, *Tribal Music of Australia* (1953), include a Yirrkala-district manikay item "Djedbang-ari". — [Australian Screen Online](https://aso.gov.au/titles/music/tribal-music-of-australia/notes/)

### Inferences
- "No toot" is a defining trait of a whole family of traditional styles (west Arnhem, Daly, Kimberley), and their instruments barely sound it; a west-Arnhem-like preset would disable the overblow and rely on pulse pattern, slight pitch inflection and voiced colouring.
- "Long meditative drone with slow vowel wander" corresponds to contemporary playing, not to any traditional style described here; Yolŋu sources explicitly distance their playing from slow sounds and swirling harmonics.
- A traditional performance is a short item (1–2 minutes in west Arnhem) with a defined start order and a defined ending, repeated many times, not one long piece.

### Gaps
- No source describing the sound of David Blanasi's mako playing in detail (mouth sounds, voicing, typical patterns) could be opened; statements about west Arnhem style here are thin and partly from encyclopedia text.
- No technique-level descriptions (in the players' or interviewers' words) were found for Smeykal, Lapaine, Zalem, Charlie McMahon or William Barton; only one-line characterisations.
- Trevor Jones's and Alice Moyle's own analyses were not accessible; the A-type/B-type account is second-hand.

## 4. How a note starts and ends in real playing

### Takeaway
In traditional playing the instrument is not "played in notes": it starts once per song item (sometimes with warm-up blips or a sustained toot), runs without a break through the item on circular breathing, and stops abruptly on cue, usually before the clapsticks' terminating pattern. I found no source describing the acoustic attack of the drone (blip, scoop, noise) precisely.

### Cited Findings
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Yolŋu tend to "start the yidaki with short notes, as if warming it up", then play a short drone before the rhythm begins. — [Yidaki Story: The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` A sustained trumpet note can introduce a song and signal the dancers that it is about to start; quick drone/toot alternations cue changes or endings. — [Yidaki Story: The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` The subtle belly-pulse "flutter" colours the introductory drone of a song. — [Yidaki Story: Pulses of Air](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/pulses-of-air)
- `[ETHNO]` *Wangga*: every item begins with an instrumental introduction that "establishes both the pitch and the tempo of the song"; before an unmeasured vocal section the didjeridu plays this introduction alone. At the end the didjeridu player finishes first, on the singer's signal (raised sticks or a glance); then the singer beats the final terminating patterns. — [Marett, Barwick & Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)
- `[ETHNO]` *Manyardi*: voice and didjeridu finish first, the sticks beat a final terminating pattern. — [ANU Press chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)
- `[ENCYC]` (snippet) *Kun-borrk*: the didjeridu nearly always starts first and most commonly stops first. — [Wikipedia: Indigenous music of Australia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunggul)
- `[ENCYC]` With circular breathing a note can be sustained "for as long as desired"; recordings of more than 40 and more than 50 minutes of continuous playing exist. — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` Breath pattern differs by style: Yolŋu take many small breaths (on the *dhirrḻ*); cheek-bellows players "play for a while, take a big breath, and then play for a while again". — [Yidaki Story: Breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) When blowing harder the lips can "blow out" and the drone is lost; lip tension must be adjusted to keep them vibrating. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: louder & quieter](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=358)

### Inferences
- Within one item there is no re-attack of the drone: what a keyboard player would treat as "new notes" are tongue/belly articulations on a continuous tone. Re-triggering an attack per key press is unlike the instrument unless the attack is itself one of the articulations (*ḏith*, *ḏup*).
- The end of the didjeridu part is a hard stop on a cue (often right after a toot figure in Yolŋu playing), not a fade; a long release tail is not supported by any source.
- A believable start has a few short "warming-up" notes or a held toot before the drone settles; a believable intro drone carries a faint flutter.

### Gaps
- No source describes the first 100–300 ms of the drone (breath noise before the buzz, pitch scoop from below, accidental flash of the overtone). This needs listening/analysis of recordings rather than literature.
- No source says how the pitch or tone behaves in the last moments before the stop, or whether traditional players drop the pitch at phrase ends (drop-jaw pitch falls are documented only as a contemporary technique).
- Length of a single unbroken exhalation in the cheek-bellows style: not found.

## 5. Loudness and timbre, and how much the drone's pitch moves

### Takeaway
Sources agree that timbre moves constantly while pitch stays put: the drone is described as one constant note, with only slight inflections (A-type) and deliberate dips from the jaw in contemporary playing. I found no source that states plainly that louder means brighter; accents are described as surges of power combined with tongue motion, and the sharpest timbre change is the one during inhalation.

### Cited Findings
- `[ACOUST]` The instrument "usually plays only one note"; the spectrogram on the page shows it at constant frequency while the formants move. — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- `[ETHNO]` In the overtone-absent (A-type) style the continuous drone has slight pitch inflections. — [aboriginalart.com.au: traditional playing](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Dropping the jaw while loosening the lips lowers the drone, more so on instruments with higher back-pressure; opening and closing the jaw gives a noticeable dip that returns when the jaw closes. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: drop jaw](https://dev.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=13338), [X8 Drums beginner's guide](https://x8drums.com/v/blog/2009/01/beginners-guide-to-didgeridoo.asp)
- `[ACOUST]` (snippet) Tongue position changes both pitch and timbre. — [UNSW: acoustics of the yidaki](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- `[TEACHER]` (snippet) Playing louder is mostly more diaphragm pressure, with lip tension adjusted so the lips keep vibrating. — [Didgeridoo Dojo: louder & quieter](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=358)
- `[YOLNGU-TEACH]` The loud moments are the single belly pulses fired with the tongue motions; the strongest is "dith" ("the surge of power"), which also briefly hints at the trumpet note. — [Yidaki Story: Pulses of Air](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/pulses-of-air), [Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)
- `[ACOUST]` Timbre "changes sharply" during inhalation; players use that and loudness changes together to mark rhythm. — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- `[ENCYC]` Vocal-tract formants "change during inhalation and exhalation" and give the instrument its recognisable sound. — [Wikipedia: Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- `[PRESS]` A recordist's tip is to boost the upper mids "to bring out the raspiness of the tone". — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)
- `[ETHNO]` (snippet) In Arnhem Land the fundamental usually lies between B and G (Knopoff, Grove Music, as quoted on a French forum). — [francedidgeridoo.com](https://francedidgeridoo.com/download/file.php?id=5581)

### Inferences
- Pitch movement in the drone is event-like (a brief inflection tied to an articulation, a jaw dip, the jump to the toot), not a slow continuous wander; a slow random drift is not described by any source, though none rules out small drift either.
- Loudness and timbre are coupled through the gesture (belly pulse + tongue transit happen together), so an accent should change level and mouth shape at once; whether level alone brightens the tone is unanswered here and belongs to the acoustics researchers.
- The inhalation should be modelled as a distinct, abrupt timbre (mouth-only resonance, typically also a level change), recurring in rhythm.

### Gaps
- No figures in cents for drone stability, for sag over a breath, or for the size of a drop-jaw bend.
- No source directly addresses "does louder mean brighter" for the drone.
- Sources disagree on octave labelling of the fundamental (Wikipedia gives a written range A2–G3, UNSW says players prefer about A1–F2); left to the acoustics notes.

## 6. What listeners and players name as the difference between real and synthesized or sampled didgeridoo

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and mostly from makers of imitations, but it points the same way: a static or looped drone is what gives an imitation away; what is missing is the player, i.e. continuous timbre movement, rhythm tied to breathing, the tone change on the in-breath, vocalisation and upper-mid rasp. No listening test or player commentary comparing real and synthetic instruments was found.

### Cited Findings
- `[PRESS]` A synth + wah-wah + short high-feedback delay "virtual" didgeridoo "sounds nothing like the real thing"; a later sampler patch was closer, but "any electronic approximation is destined to be pretty rough" because the instrument is "such an overtly organic instrument". — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)
- `[PRESS]` The same author lists what matters: the instrument is "played very rhythmically" (a rhythm is preferable to a plain drone); cheek and mouth movement act as a moving filter; the tone changes between blowing and drawing breath, so breathing should follow the rhythm; animal/bird impressions and spoken words; long crossfade loops are needed to "capture the movement of timbre that characterises the digeridoo"; upper-mid "raspiness". — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)
- `[VENDOR]` (snippet) Soundiron's sampled *Didgeridoos* library states it is "not intended to represent or replace the authentic realism, cultural importance and soul of traditional aboriginal musicianship". — [Soundiron: Didgeridoos](https://soundiron.com/products/didgeridoos)
- `[VENDOR]` (snippet) Fluffy Audio's *DidgHybridoo* is sold on having a vowel control and on avoiding "fixed phrases or uncontrollable drones", i.e. the vendor names fixed loops and static drones as the weakness of earlier sampled instruments. — [KVR: DidgHybridoo](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/didghybridoo-by-fluffyaudio/details)
- `[ACOUST]` (snippet) An attempt to drive a real didgeridoo with artificial air sources concluded that the buzzing lips "were not appropriately modelled" and the result "did not sound like a didgeridoo". — [University of Alberta thesis/paper](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/a39522d3-9062-4622-b68d-c97c5e68132a/download/3b03880b-21b7-4b85-995e-69af0df67387)
- `[ACOUST]` Producing strong, clear formants is what takes a human player time to learn (strong vocal-tract resonances); beginners get the basic sound quickly but not the formants or the breathing. — [UNSW: physics of the didgeridoo](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- `[COLLECTOR]` Some Aboriginal players compare contemporary (non-traditional) playing to aeroplane noises, i.e. to a continuous swirling drone without the pulsed accents of traditional technique. — [iDIDJ: Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)

### Inferences
- The recurring complaint is about performance, not spectrum: static drones and fixed loops. Strong, fast-moving formants, the abrupt in-breath timbre, accents that are surges with a tongue gesture, and short toot stabs are the features the sources tie to a real player.
- For a listener who knows traditional playing, a slow vowel LFO over a steady drone resembles exactly the "aeroplane" sound that Aboriginal players are reported to distance themselves from; realism for that listener depends on the pulsed tongue/belly articulation.
- A synthesizer that markets a "traditional" sound should take care with wording; even a sample-library vendor disclaims representing traditional musicianship, and Yolŋu sources state that some yiḏaki parts are restricted and that animal sounds belong to particular songs.

### Gaps
- No blind listening tests, no player or listener forum threads, and no reviews comparing sampled/synthesized didgeridoos with real playing were found; the vendor statements are marketing copy.
- Nothing was found on how specific commercial instruments handle breath cycles or re-attacks, so no benchmark of the state of the art is available from this research.
