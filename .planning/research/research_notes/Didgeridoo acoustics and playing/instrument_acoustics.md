# Acoustics of the didgeridoo (didjeridu, yidaki) as an instrument: bore, resonances and radiated sound

Scope: the tube itself, not the player. Lips, vocal tract and technique appear only where a source ties them to the bore.

Conventions used below:

- f1, f2 … = frequencies of the 1st, 2nd … maxima of the input impedance measured at the mouth end; Z1, Z2 … = their magnitudes; Z0 = characteristic impedance; Q1 = quality factor of the first peak.
- Impedances are in MPa·s·m⁻³ ("acoustic megohms").
- Semitone values in brackets marked "my conversion" are 12·log2(ratio), computed by me from the ratio the source gives.
- "Peer-reviewed" = JASA / Acoustics Australia / conference proceedings of the UNSW–ANU group. "Maker/seller claim" and "amateur" are marked as such.
- Three items are marked "my reading of the figure": the source prints a graph, not numbers, and the values are my approximate readings (±5 dB or about ±30 %).

## 1. Dimensions and bore shapes of real instruments

### Takeaway
A traditional instrument is a termite-hollowed eucalypt tube 1.2–1.5 m long (about 1 m in Central Australia), about 30 mm bore at the lips (range 25–60 mm) opening irregularly to 40–150 mm at the far end, with 5–10 mm walls: acoustically a heavily truncated, rough, irregular cone, neither a cylinder nor a complete cone. PVC and bamboo instruments are cylinders (typically 38 mm bore), which is the limiting case with the strongest, most harmonic (1:3:5) resonances.

### Cited Findings

Length
- "Didjeridus from Central Australia are typically about one metre in length, while those from Arnhem Land are usually about 1.5 metres long. The longer didjeridus are now generally preferred because they allow a greater range of musical effects." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Typical length 1.2–1.5 m; "different cultural groups have different styles". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); same figure in [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf) and [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- General reference figure: 1 to 3 m long, "most are around 1.2 m". — [Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo) (tertiary source)

Bore diameters
- Blowing end about 30 mm internal diameter, free end about 50 mm, "though all these dimensions vary significantly from one instrument to another, even among those by the same maker"; average wall thickness "usually 5 to 10 mm"; the free end "is often given a slight extra flare by internal scraping". — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Bore "usually somewhat flared from about 30 to 50 mm at the blowing end to about 40 to 150 mm at the open end". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Blowing end about 30 mm, open end about 50 mm, "although some instruments flare to more than 100 mm diameter". — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- The 38 measured instruments (termite-eaten eucalypt, made by indigenous craftsmen near Kuranda, North Queensland; not instruments of traditional custodians): internal diameter at the mouth end 30 to 60 mm (measured before the wax was applied), flare ratio (output diameter / input diameter) 1.0 to 2.4. The output end is "often asymmetric"; its effective diameter was taken as the geometric mean of the largest and smallest internal diameter. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html); method in [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Measured characteristic impedance Z0 of those 38 instruments: about 0.2 to 1 MPa·s·m⁻³, "the expected values for cylindrical pipes with diameters in the approximate range 25–50 mm". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

Three measured Arnhem Land instruments (Fletcher's Table 1; instruments 1 and 2 are by the same maker)

| Length L | d1 (blowing end) | d2 (open end) | Drone f1 | Pitch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 159 cm | 31 mm | 36 mm | 60 Hz | B1 |
| 144 cm | 26 mm | 60 mm | 80 Hz | E2 |
| 149 cm | 30 mm | 40 mm | 64 Hz | C2 |

— [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)

Bore shape and surface
- "It is usually neither a cylinder nor a nearly complete cone and its resonances form a 'stretched' quasi-harmonic series." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- "The overall internal shape of a typical didjeridu is a heavily truncated cone, with a highly irregular surface." Angle and truncation vary widely among instruments. — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- The termite galleries are cleared with long sticks and the inside "rubbed to moderate smoothness"; the tube has "enhanced wall losses because of roughness and porosity". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Unlike other lip-valve instruments there is no cup mouthpiece and no constriction: the bore is "relatively wide … along its entire length", comparable in diameter to the player's vocal tract. — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)
- External appearance does not reveal the bore: instruments graded from "high concert" to "second class" look alike; "the detailed bore profiles are not known even to the experimenters". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

Regional styles of bore
- Citing Knopoff 1997: clans of Eastern Arnhem Land (including the Yolngu), whose style uses rhythmic articulation and higher tube modes for accents, prefer "almost cylindrical didjeridus"; clans of Western Arnhem Land make little use of the higher modes and prefer "didjeridus with a flaring bore profile". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- The West Arnhem "mako" is "usually shorter" than the yidaki and sounds "slightly fuller and richer … but without the 'overtone' note". — [Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo) (tertiary; its source is a seller's glossary)

Yolngu yidaki as listed by a specialist seller (seller's own measurements; treat as maker/seller data)
- Archive of 43 sold yidaki by named Yolngu makers (Djalu Gurruwiwi and others): lengths 45 in to 67 in (114–170 cm), mostly 51–62 in (130–157 cm); "bell" 3 in to 5.5 in (7.6–14 cm), one of 9 in (23 cm); mouthpiece 1 1/16 in to 1 5/8 in (2.7–4.1 cm), most often 1.25 in (3.2 cm). The page does not say whether "bell" is the inside or outside diameter. — [Ginger Root Rhythms, sold yidaki archive](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm)

Plastic, bamboo and modern instruments
- UNSW's "model yidaki" for acoustic measurements: cylindrical PVC pipe, length 1210 mm, inner diameter 30 mm; for optical work a square plexiglass pipe 1220 mm long, 38 mm internal width. "Experienced players reported that both model instruments played moderately well." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Control instruments in the quality study: nine PVC tubes of 38 mm diameter and different lengths (A1 to F2), and three PVC tubes of 25, 38 and 50 mm at the same pitch C2. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- "50 mm appears to be a practical upper limit [of bore diameter] for players with conventional facial geometry." — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- Non-traditional instruments are made from split-and-rejoined hardwood, bamboo, glass, fibreglass, metal, agave, clay, resin, PVC and carbon fibre, with "an upper inside diameter of around 3 cm down to a bell end of anywhere between 5 and 20 cm". Early reports describe bamboo instruments about 3 ft long. — [Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- Maker's rules of thumb (maker claim, unmeasured): mouthpiece 2.8–3.2 cm, "anything larger than 3.5 cm makes the drone difficult to hold"; keep the first 30 cm narrow (3–4 cm); bell flare expanding to 8–15 cm; walls thicker than 1.0 cm. — [Didgitaldoo design cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)

### Inferences
- The numbers describe one family with a wide spread: mouth end 26–60 mm (centre near 30 mm), far end 36–150 mm, flare ratio 1.0–2.4 in the measured Queensland set and up to about 3–4 for wide-belled yidaki in the seller list (3.2 cm mouthpiece to 10–14 cm bell, if the bell figure is an inside diameter).
- A cylinder (PVC, bamboo) is the edge of that family, not its centre: flare ratio 1.0 occurred in the measured set, but most instruments flare.
- The two statements about East Arnhem instruments do not sit easily together: Fletcher et al. (via Knopoff) call the Yolngu preference "almost cylindrical", while the seller's yidaki have large bells and toot intervals well below a twelfth (see question 4), which requires real taper. A narrow, near-cylindrical upper section with a flare lower down would reconcile them, but I found no measured bore profile of a traditional yidaki to confirm it.

### Gaps
- No measured internal bore profile (diameter against distance) of any traditional instrument was found; Smith et al. state that the profiles of their 38 instruments are unknown. Amir's bore and resonance measurements (2001, 2004) and Knopoff's regional data were not accessible.
- No peer-reviewed dimensions for bamboo or "tourist" instruments were found; the only figures are the Wikipedia and maker statements above.
- No measurement of how much the wax ring narrows the mouth opening was found; the UNSW diameters were taken before the wax was applied.

## 2. Drone pitch: range, common keys, how length sets pitch

### Takeaway
The drone sits at or just above the first bore resonance: typically 55–80 Hz (about 70 Hz), with measured instruments spanning 50–105 Hz, that is A1 to G2; the commonest keys are C2 to F2. A cylinder follows f = c/4L, and flare raises the pitch for a given length by a factor of 1.06 to 1.38.

### Cited Findings

Range
- Drone "in the frequency range 55 to 80 Hz", "usually about 70 Hz". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- "Sealed at the lip end and open at the other, its lowest resonance is typically 50 to 80 Hz." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- 38 measured instruments: nominal playing pitches A1 (55 Hz) to G2 (98 Hz); measured f1 "from approximately 50 to 105 Hz". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Three Arnhem Land instruments: 60 Hz (B1), 80 Hz (E2), 64 Hz (C2). — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)

Common keys
- Distribution in the 38-instrument set, which was chosen to cover the shop's normal range: five each at F2, E2 and C2; four each at D#2 and D2; three each at G2, C#2, B1 and A#1; two at A1. (These counts add up to 37, not 38, in the paper.) — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Players "seem to prefer instruments in the pitch range approximately A1 to F2. (For typical male voices, pitches in the middle of this range suit the heterodyne effects.)" — [UNSW, Didgeridoo acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)
- Within A1–G2, pitch had no significant correlation with ranked quality, for the 38 instruments and for nine PVC pipes. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- A large shop lists "the most common keys" as the twelve from A up to G# (seller statement). — [Didjshop, musical keys](https://www.didjshop.com/shop1/didj_musical_keys.php)
- Drone keys of 43 Yolngu yidaki in a seller's archive (my tally of the listing): E ×9, D ×9, F ×7, Eb/D# ×7, F# ×4, C# ×2, C ×2, Ab ×1, and two listed between keys (F#–G, Eb–E). — [Ginger Root Rhythms, sold yidaki archive](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm)
- "Traditional makers and players seem to have little concern with either the drone frequency or the interval to the second mode"; for Western music an instrument of matching pitch is selected, or the recording is pitch-shifted. — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)

How length sets pitch
- Cylinder closed at the lips: resonances at fn = (n − ½)·c/2L′, i.e. c/4L′, 3c/4L′, 5c/4L′ …, with acoustic length L′ = L + 0.3·d2 (open-end correction). — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Truncated cone (d1 at the lips, d2 at the open end): impedance maxima at kn·L′ = nπ − arctan(kn·x1), with x1 = d1·L/(d2 − d1) the distance from the cone's apex to the blowing end and kn = 2πfn/c. — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html); same relation in [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- "For the range of end diameters found in the typical didjeridus of Table 1, this fundamental-mode frequency is raised by a factor between about 1.06 and 1.38 relative to a cylindrical tube of the same length." Example: the second instrument "is only 3 percent shorter than the third, but its fundamental frequency is 25 percent higher because of its large flare". — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- PVC rule used by makers: f = c/4L with c = 344 m/s at 20 °C; key of C (65.40 Hz) needs 1.315 m; a 1.05 m pipe gives 81.90 Hz, close to E (82.41 Hz, exact length 1.04 m). — [Didjshop, Physics of the didgeridoo](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidj.html) (seller page; simple formula without end correction)
- The 1210 mm × 30 mm PVC pipe was played "at about 70 Hz"; the 1220 mm square pipe had a lip period of 14.3 ms. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Amateur measurement on one termite instrument: 63 in (1.60 m) long, cylinder formula gives 53.75 Hz, measured fundamental about 63 Hz. — [B. Hammond, The Physics of Dreamtime](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidjDreamtime.html) (student paper hosted by a shop; not peer-reviewed)

### Inferences
- Equal-tempered frequencies (A4 = 440 Hz) for the keys in question, as standard values not taken from a source: A1 55.0, A#1 58.3, B1 61.7, C2 65.4, C#2 69.3, D2 73.4, D#2 77.8, E2 82.4, F2 87.3, F#2 92.5, G2 98.0 Hz.
- Two samples give two centres: the Queensland shop set is spread evenly over C2–F2 (65–87 Hz), while the Yolngu yidaki in the seller archive cluster on D, Eb, E and F (73–87 Hz) with few at C or below. A default drone between C2 and E2 is central for both.
- The 1.60 m amateur instrument sounding 17 % above its cylinder value (63 against 53.75 Hz) falls inside Fletcher's 1.06–1.38 range, so "length alone" underestimates the pitch of a flared instrument by one to nearly six semitones (factors 1.06 to 1.38).
- I solved Fletcher's truncated-cone equation for his three instruments (c = 343 m/s): f1 = 57, 80 and 64 Hz against the measured 60, 80 and 64 Hz. The simple model therefore predicts the drone of a real instrument to within a semitone from three numbers (L, d1, d2).

### Gaps
- No statistical survey of drone pitch in traditional (pre-commercial) instruments was found; both samples above are commercial selections.
- The maker's cheat sheet contradicts itself on cones ("cones need to be longer than cylinders to hit the same deep notes" and "a strongly conical didgeridoo can actually play a deeper note than a cylinder of the exact same length"); only the first agrees with Fletcher's formula. I did not use it.

## 3. Resonances and input impedance

### Takeaway
The first impedance peak is tall and sharp (Z1 5–40 MPa·s·m⁻³, 20–70 times Z0, Q1 12–86) and carries the drone; the higher peaks are spaced like a compressed 1:3:5 series (f2/f1 measured 2.6–3.0, "harmonicity" 0.72–1.0) and fade with frequency at a rate that differs sixfold between instruments. Because the peaks are not harmonically placed, the drone's low harmonics get little help from the tube (weak 2nd–5th harmonics, no systematic odd/even pattern), whereas a cylinder reinforces the odd harmonics below about 1 kHz.

### Cited Findings

Measured on 38 termite-made instruments (impedance head at the mouth end, 50–3000 Hz, 1.346 Hz resolution) — all from [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

| Quantity | Measured range | Note |
|---|---|---|
| f1 | about 50 to 105 Hz | no correlation with quality |
| Z0 (geometric mean of first maximum and first minimum) | about 0.2 to 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ | lower = better (p = 5×10⁻⁶) |
| Z1 | about 5 to 40 MPa·s·m⁻³ | lower = better (p = 1×10⁻⁴) |
| Z1/Z0 | about 20 to 70 | higher = better (p = 0.04) |
| Q1 | 12 to 86 | higher = better (p = 0.02) |
| f2/f1 | about 2.6 to 3.0 | no correlation with quality |
| Harmonicity (slope of fn against (2n−1)·f1, first six peaks) | 0.72 to 1.0 | 1 = perfect cylinder; no correlation |
| Z2/Z1 | about 0.2 to 1.4 | no correlation |
| λ in Zi = Z1·exp[−λ(fi − f1)], fitted to the first 3–6 peaks | 1 to 6 kHz⁻¹ | no correlation |
| Largest peak between 1 and 2 kHz | ≤ 1 for the best, ≥ 3 for the worst | strongest predictor (p = 3×10⁻⁷) |

- The less formal UNSW account of the same data gives the first peak "with one exception … between 8 and 30 MPa·s·m⁻³". — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- "In general, the magnitudes of the first few peaks in the spectrum decrease monotonically with increasing frequency." In only one of 38 instruments (#5) was Z2 significantly higher than Z1; Amir's (2004) instrument E1 had Z3 noticeably higher than Z1 and Z2. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Z0 scaled with din⁻² with a fitted slope of 190 ± 30 against about 500 expected for a cylinder (same units), i.e. roughly 0.4 of the impedance of a cylinder with the same mouth diameter. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- My reading of Fig. 2 of that paper (three instruments, log scale): best instrument Z1 about 10–20, peaks falling to about 3 near 0.3 kHz, about 1 near 0.8 kHz and below about 0.6 above 1.2 kHz, where the curve is nearly structureless (Z0 about 0.3); middle-ranked instrument Z1 about 15 with peaks of about 1–3 persisting to 3 kHz; worst instrument Z1 about 20–30 with peaks of about 3–5 near 1 kHz and 1–3 up to 3 kHz (Z0 about 0.6). — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

Where the peaks lie: cylinder, cone, real instrument
- Cylinder closed at one end: mode frequencies in the sequence 1, 3, 5 … (× c/4L′). Complete cone of the same length: 2, 4, 6 … (× c/4L′, i.e. 1:2:3). A didjeridu of small flare lies between the two, "a stretched quasiharmonic sequence"; a real instrument "does not have quite this regular form, though the behavior of its lower modes is generally well approximated by the flared-tube relation". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Flare raises the lower modes, and the fundamental most: "all the mode intervals are compressed. For moderate flare, only the lowest mode frequency is significantly affected." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- "Because the truncation is large, the mode frequencies follow a pattern almost like 1, 3, 5, …, except that the lowest mode, particularly, is significantly raised in frequency." — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- A horn on the end "contract[s]" the frequency ratios of the peaks "compared to those of a simple pipe", because its effective length is longer for higher frequencies. — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- Brass instruments have "a dozen or so impedance maxima, several of these having sharp peaks and nearly all falling close to a harmonic series"; the didjeridu's harmonics "need only occasionally and 'accidentally' coincide with the impedance peaks". — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)

Losses and the size of the peaks
- Characteristic impedance of a tube of diameter d: Z0 = 4ρc/πd². Wall attenuation coefficient α ≈ 10⁻⁵·β·ω^½/d (SI units), with β "about 2.4 for perfectly smooth walls, but may become much larger than this for the rough walls characteristic of a naturally produced didjeridu"; the authors' model (1.3 m long, 40 mm diameter) uses β = 12. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Bore of 30–50 mm at the lips makes the characteristic impedance "lower by an order of magnitude than that of most of the members of the lip-driven musical instrument family"; with the rough walls, the impedance maxima are "rather lower" than in other wind instruments. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- The smooth 30 mm PVC pipe "has peaks in this frequency range [around 1.5 kHz] of 3 to 10 MPa·s·m⁻³", about the same as a raised-tongue vocal tract (peak of about 8 at 1.5 kHz; tract maxima 2–10 between 1 and 3 kHz). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- "In a widely flaring tube, overtones other than the first are rather difficult to produce because of the rapidly decreasing quality factor of the tube resonances." — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Felt strips inside a PVC pipe lowered the resonance frequencies (and the playing pitch) and had "a dramatic effect on Z(f) above 1 kHz". Large horns left a "nearly complete lack of resonances for wavelengths less than about 0.5 m". — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)

What the resonances do to the harmonics of the drone
- "The sound spectrum of the didjeridu … is strictly harmonic. The fact that the upper modes of the pipe are not in harmonic relation to the fundamental affects only the strength of certain harmonics." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Cylinder: inside the mouth the harmonic amplitudes "fall almost monotonically", but "the spectrum of radiated sound … has relatively weak second, fourth, and sixth harmonics", because the 3rd and 5th harmonics fall near bore resonances "and so are radiated efficiently". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Cylinder, measured (1210 mm × 30 mm PVC, played about 3 Hz above f1 at about 70 Hz): odd harmonics dominate at low frequencies; approaching 1 kHz "the odd-even difference in the sound spectrum disappears, because in this frequency range the harmonics fall almost midway between resonances"; around 1.5 kHz "it is the even harmonics that benefit"; in a low-tongue example the coincidence boosts odd harmonics again around 2.5 kHz. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Cylinder, model (1.3 m × 40 mm, played at 1.03·f1): "an actual transfer of emphasis [from odd to even harmonics] takes place at about 1000 Hz and another one in the reverse direction at about 3500 Hz". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Flared bore: "there are no clear frequency regions in which even or odd harmonics dominates. In addition, the low harmonics above the fundamental are generally weaker because their frequencies do not approximate those of tube resonances." — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- "The transfer function … has maxima at the same frequencies as does the input impedance at the lip end of the tube": a harmonic that lands on an impedance peak is radiated strongly. — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Amir (2003, 2004), as reported: a characteristic "hole" in the sound spectrum, "the first few overtones above the fundamental are weak". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- "Harmonic coincidence": the 1–2 kHz band holds the 20th to 30th harmonics, so "changes in the playing frequency of only a couple of percent (a fraction of a semitone) can produce or remove" the coincidence of a harmonic with a bore resonance; it yields "broad and weak" formants, smaller than those made by the vocal tract. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf); [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- A second resonance close to 3·f1 pulls on the playing pitch: instrument #5 (the only one with Z2 > Z1) was played at 98 Hz, between f1 (102 Hz) and f2/3 (92 Hz). — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

### Inferences
- Bandwidth of the first resonance: Δf = f1/Q1, so for a 70 Hz drone the measured Q1 of 12–86 means a −3 dB width of about 0.8 to 6 Hz. The "body" of a real instrument is therefore a very narrow resonance, 26–37 dB above the general impedance level (Z1/Z0 = 20–70).
- Fall-off of the peaks: λ = 1–6 kHz⁻¹ in an exponential law equals 8.7·λ dB per kHz, i.e. the peak heights drop by about 9 dB per kHz in the most resonant instruments and about 52 dB per kHz in the most damped. Good instruments are at the damped end (peaks ≤ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ above 1 kHz).
- Peak spacing: neighbouring maxima are about c/2L apart, 115–145 Hz for a 1.2–1.5 m tube, so 7–9 instrument resonances lie between 1 and 2 kHz. They are individually narrow and weak in a good instrument; nothing in the measurements suggests a broad formant-like band belonging to the tube itself in that range.
- Wall-loss check: Fletcher's α formula gives, for a 40 mm tube at 70 Hz, Q = ω/(2cα) of about 51 for smooth walls (β = 2.4) and about 10 for β = 12. The measured Q1 range of 12–86 spans both, which supports β of roughly 2 to 12 as the plausible range for real instruments.
- Harmonicity of 0.72–1.0 means the n-th peak sits at about H·(2n−1)·f1: the second at 2.2–3.0·f1 and the third at 3.6–5.0·f1 on that fit. Only near H = 1 (a cylinder) do the peaks coincide with the 3rd and 5th harmonics; at H = 0.85 the second peak (2.55·f1) falls between the 2nd and 3rd harmonics and helps neither.
- Consequence for an odd/even balance: strong odd-harmonic dominance below about 1 kHz is the signature of a cylinder (PVC, bamboo), not of a termite-made instrument. A flared instrument has an essentially unpatterned low spectrum with a strong fundamental and weakened 2nd to about 5th harmonics.

### Gaps
- Q factors and bandwidths of the second and higher peaks are not reported anywhere I could read; only Q1 and the decay constant λ of the peak heights are.
- The individual peak frequencies of the 38 instruments are not tabulated; only the ranges of f2/f1 and of the harmonicity slope are given.
- Amir's and Caussé's measured resonance tables (eight instruments each) were not accessible.

## 4. Overblown notes ("toots", trumpet notes, horn)

### Takeaway
The toot sounds at the second bore resonance, between an octave and a twelfth above the drone: peer-reviewed statements give 2.5–3 times the drone frequency and measured f2/f1 of 2.6–3.0 (16.5–19.0 semitones), while a seller's list of 43 Yolngu yidaki clusters at 15–17 semitones (minor tenth to eleventh). A third-resonance toot is rare; it needs 2–4 times the blowing pressure and is used only as a brief accent. No measurement of the toot's spectrum was found.

### Cited Findings

Which resonance, how it is used
- "Occasional accents are also introduced by momentary leaps to the second mode of the air column, at a frequency between about 2.5 and 3 times the drone frequency, depending upon the shape of the instrument bore." (= 15.9 to 19.0 semitones, my conversion) — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- "Very brief soundings of the second resonance, at a pitch typically from a ninth to an eleventh above the drone note, are sometimes used to interrupt the low note and to create another complex rhythm"; also brief "toots" to end a performance. "'Bugling', i.e. rapidly sounding the first, second and third resonance in sequence, is possible, but seems to be very rare in traditional performance." — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- "Some players can produce the third mode and even higher modes, but they are rarely used. Indeed, even the second mode is only employed for brief accents, and not as a sustained tone." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Overblown notes are "not usual in traditional playing in Western Arnhem Land, though [they are] used in the East". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)

Interval above the drone
- Measured ratio of the first two impedance peaks, 38 termite-made instruments: f2/f1 "from approximately 2.6 to 3.0" (= 16.5 to 19.0 semitones, my conversion). — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- One forked termite-made instrument, four playing configurations: "it was possible to play the higher resonance at frequency f2, where the ratio 2.83 < f2/f1 < 2.93" (= 18.0 to 18.6 semitones, my conversion). — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2007](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/SchneiderEtAl.pdf)
- Cylinder: the first two notes are "approximately a musical twelfth apart … On didjeridus made by termites, however, the bore is usually somewhat flared, so the interval is typically a tenth or an eleventh." For a cylinder the second and third resonances would be "roughly a musical twelfth … and a major seventeenth (two octaves plus a major third) above the lowest note. Usually, however, the intervals are somewhat smaller than this." — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- "The second mode therefore sounds appreciably below the pitch of the expected musical twelfth." — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- For the three Table 1 instruments the second mode is predicted between "about a tone flat of a perfect twelfth" and "a little less than a semitone flat"; "the greater the flare, the flatter the second mode appears relative to the drone fundamental". The second-mode frequencies of those instruments "were not recorded". (The paper prints these intervals as "1.30" and "1.43" against "1.50" for a twelfth; the unit is not stated and is not a plain frequency ratio.) — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- The same convention appears in Fletcher's 2003 paper: the second mode has a "frequency a little less than 1.5 times that of the fundamental (because of the tube flare)". Read as a frequency ratio this is wrong for a tube closed at the lips (a cylinder gives 3); it only makes sense as "about an octave and a half". Do not quote 1.5 as the toot ratio. — [Fletcher 2003, SMAC paper (hosted by Didjshop)](https://www.didjshop.com/austrAboriginalMusicInstruments.htm)
- Tertiary summary: the second resonance "is usually around an 11th higher than the fundamental frequency (a frequency ratio somewhat less than 3:1)". — [Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)
- Seller's list of 43 Yolngu yidaki, each given as "X drone / Y trumpet" by note name (seller's ear or tuner; octave not stated). Taking the toot to lie between an octave and a twelfth above the drone, my tally of the intervals is:

Second toot (third resonance)
- Three yidaki in the same list give a second trumpet note: E drone / A / F#, C drone / F / Db, D drone / F# / D#. — [Ginger Root Rhythms, sold yidaki archive](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm) (seller data)
- For a cylinder the third resonance is a major seventeenth above the drone (ratio 5); real instruments give less. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)

Effort and timbre
- "A didjeridu player typically uses a pressure of about 1–2 kPa (10–20 cm water gauge pressure) for the drone note and about 4–5 kPa to produce the second mode"; the second mode needs "a much higher lip tension". — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- "If one of the upper pipe modes is sounded instead of the fundamental, then this sound will itself be accompanied by its own set of harmonics." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- A narrow, nearly cylindrical bore "will have strong lower resonances in nearly harmonic relationship and it will be relatively easy to produce the higher modes"; in a widely flaring tube they are "rather difficult to produce". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Players' and sellers' vocabulary for the toot: "trumpet", "horn", "hoot"; seller descriptions such as "horn is bright but still balanced" and "bright horn tone". — [Ginger Root Rhythms, sold yidaki archive](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm) (seller's subjective descriptions)
- Maker claim: on a cylinder the toots "are very high and difficult to reach"; on a conical bore "the toots are lower in pitch, making them easier to play and integrate into rhythms". — [Didgitaldoo design cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)

### Inferences
- A default of 16 semitones (major tenth, ratio 2.52) is inside the real range but toward its lower side for termite-made instruments in general: the UNSW impedance data start at 16.5 semitones and run to 19.0, and the literature's "typical" values are a tenth or an eleventh (15–17). It matches Yolngu yidaki well, where 15, 16 and 17 semitones account for 33 of 43 instruments and 15 is the mode.
- The physically meaningful range for a toot-interval control is 12 to 19 semitones (complete cone to cylinder). Values above 19 do not occur on a simple flared tube; values of 12–14 mean a strongly conical bore.
- For a cylinder model (PVC, bamboo) the interval should be 19 semitones (ratio 3.0).
- The interval and the drone's low-harmonic strength are linked by the bore: an instrument whose toot is a twelfth (cylinder) is the one with a strong 3rd harmonic in the drone; one whose toot is a tenth has its second resonance near 2.4–2.5·f1, between the 2nd and 3rd harmonics, and so a weaker 3rd harmonic.
- I solved Fletcher's truncated-cone equation for his three Arnhem Land instruments: f2/f1 = 2.85, 2.34 and 2.72, i.e. 18.1, 14.7 and 17.3 semitones for flare ratios d2/d1 of 1.16, 2.31 and 1.33. This reproduces the whole observed spread from three plain measurements and shows that a 2.3-fold flare already brings the toot down to a minor tenth.
- Second toot: taking the seller's second trumpet notes as lying two octaves and a little above the drone, they are 25–26 semitones above it (ratio about 4.2–4.5) and 8–9 semitones above the first toot (ratio about 1.6–1.7). A cylinder would give 27.9 semitones above the drone and 8.8 semitones (5:3) between toots, so the spacing between first and second toot stays close to a sixth while both drop with flare. This rests on three instruments and an assumed octave, but it agrees with the model: Fletcher's equation for his third instrument (flare ratio 1.33) gives f2/f1 = 2.72 and f3/f1 = 4.49, i.e. 17.3 and 26.0 semitones, the same pattern as the seller's "E drone / A / F#".
- The played interval need not equal f2/f1 exactly: the drone is played about 2–3 % (0.3–0.5 semitone) above f1, and the offset for the toot is not reported.
- A brighter toot is physically plausible on two counts in the sources (higher blowing pressure sharpens the flow pulse; the toot's harmonics start at 150–250 Hz, where radiation is more efficient and the ear more sensitive), but this is reasoning, not measurement.

### Gaps
- No measured spectrum, level or attack time of a toot was found in any source; all statements about its timbre are players' and sellers' descriptions.
- The octave of the seller's trumpet notes is my assumption; an interval of 12 could in principle be a unison-class error or a different octave. No peer-reviewed table of played drone and toot frequencies exists in the papers I read (the UNSW team recorded "the pitch of the first two playing resonances" of the 38 instruments but published only the impedance ratio).
- Third-resonance frequencies (f3/f1) are not published for real instruments except through the harmonicity slope.

## 5. The radiated spectrum of the drone

### Takeaway
The drone is strictly harmonic with dozens of harmonics: the lip source falls at about 12 dB per octave, radiation adds about 6 dB per octave up to roughly 3 kHz, and the tube weakens the harmonics that miss its resonances. The fundamental and first few harmonics are the strongest components but are not loud to the ear (hearing falls off below about 300 Hz); the band the ear treats as characteristic is 1–2 kHz (harmonics 20–30), where player-made formants at about 1.5–2 kHz and antiformants about 20 dB deep appear. No calibrated radiated level was found.

### Cited Findings

Source and radiation
- With lips that just close once per cycle, the flow waveform "gives a spectral envelope which is initially nearly constant and then declines at about 12 dB/octave". The tube's resistance "is less for the upper harmonics than for the resonant fundamental, except for accidental near-coincidences with higher horn resonances". "The transfer function between flow spectrum and acoustic radiation rises at 6 dB/octave at low frequencies and is then flat above about 3 kHz for the didjeridu horn." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- Measured lip-opening area (high-speed video, PVC and plexiglass pipes): the lips "are closed for about half of each cycle"; the harmonic amplitudes of the opening area follow approximately n⁻²; in the low-tongue case the harmonics above the 5th fall "even more rapidly than n⁻²". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Radiation impedance of the open end (SI units): about 7×10⁻⁴·ω² + 0.5jω/d2 at low frequency, tending to ρc/A2 ≈ 500/d2² at high frequency. Radiated power at each harmonic = radiation resistance × |flow at the open end|². — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- The closed lips for part of each cycle "enhance the harmonic content of the flow waveform and thus of the radiated sound", unlike a trumpeter's nearly sinusoidal lip motion. — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)

Measured spectra
- Radiated sound of the 1210 mm × 30 mm PVC pipe, microphone on axis 125 mm from the end, high-tongue drone at about 70 Hz: strong harmonics, "odd harmonics dominate"; formants at about 1.5–2 kHz with minima at the frequencies of the tract's impedance maxima (about 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz in the example). — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- My reading of Fig. 4(c) of that paper (levels relative to the strongest component): harmonics within about 0 to −15 dB up to about 400 Hz; about −20 dB near 500 Hz; about −35 dB near 1 kHz; trough of about −55 to −60 dB near 1.3–1.5 kHz; formant peak of about −30 to −35 dB near 1.7–1.9 kHz; trough of about −50 dB near 2.1 kHz; second hump of about −35 to −40 dB near 2.4 kHz; about −50 dB at 3 kHz. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- My reading of Fig. 1 of the ISMA paper (low-resolution print, 0–4 kHz): "uninflected drone" — the low harmonics stand about 30–35 dB above the level reached by the harmonics beyond 1.5–2 kHz, with a steady fall through the first 1.5 kHz; "nasal drone" (tongue raised) — a formant band between about 2 and 3 kHz whose strongest harmonics rise to about the level of the low harmonics. — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- Tract impedance peaks reduce the flow, and hence the radiated level, at their frequencies "by typically 20 dB". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Formant examples on real instruments: "a pronounced formant band at about 1500 Hz", shifted to "about 2.2 kHz" by constricting the mouth, and "some evidence for a lower vocal-tract formant at about 500 Hz"; the mouth resonator can be tuned "from about 500 Hz to about 3 kHz" and is damped enough that "its bandwidth encompasses the frequencies of several harmonics". — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)
- High-tongue playing "produces a strong formant between about 1.5 and 2 kHz"; a typical case has antiformants at about 1.5 and 2.0 kHz and "a prominent formant … at about 1.8 kHz". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)

How many harmonics matter, and which band the ear uses
- "Human hearing sensitivity declines rapidly below about 300 Hz. Consequently, despite their relatively large amplitude, the fundamental and lower harmonics are not very loud. Further, they vary little during playing. The formants, on the other hand, occur at frequencies in the range of maximum sensitivity of the ear." — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- "The formants that give the didjeridu its characteristic sound fall in the range roughly 1–2 kHz", which "corresponds roughly to the 20th to 30th harmonic of the normal drone note". Formants over 1–3 kHz are salient "presumably because this includes the range of the second formant (F2)" of speech. — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Amir's quality criterion, as reported: "based upon the levels of harmonics 2 to 5 relative to that of the fundamental, a didjeridu that is rated excellent by players having low levels for these harmonics". — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- Players' rankings suggest they "preferred a dominant fundamental in the sound and weak second and other low harmonics. Weak low harmonics would lead to greater subjective prominence of the harmonics in the varying formants." — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)
- Amir 2004 (abstract): eight instruments of different quality were recorded across a range of timbres; "the random nature of the instrument bore is actually conducive to creating its typical timbre". — [Amir 2004, abstract](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/1170716.htm)
- Press account of Amir's result (journalistic, not checked against the paper): "the second to eighth harmonic overtones are gone", leaving a "gap" so that "the fundamental and ninth overtone together sound like … two separate instruments". — [Ingeniøren, 27 Oct 2004](https://ing.dk/artikel/termitter-giver-den-australske-didjeridoo-dens-fantastiske-lyd)
- Amateur spectrum of one 1.60 m termite instrument (63 Hz drone): overtones "in decreasing intensity", plus a component near 300 Hz "significantly louder than its neighbors in all the tones … the loudest component besides the drone fundamental" and a raised one near 170 Hz, both unaffected by mouth shape and so attributed by the author to the instrument. — [B. Hammond, The Physics of Dreamtime](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidjDreamtime.html) (student paper; not peer-reviewed)

Level
- Inside the player's mouth at comfortable playing level: "between about 130 and 145 dB with respect to 20 μPa". — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- An earlier UNSW page says the level in the mouth "can be 100 dBA". — [UNSW, Physics of the didgeridoo (yidaki)](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidaki.html)
- Loudness rises with flare and with horn size: "highly flared instruments are usually louder"; with added horns "the loudness increased with horn size"; felt-damped pipes "were noticeably less loud". — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf); [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)

### Inferences
- First-order spectral slope from Fletcher's two statements: source about −12 dB/octave plus radiation +6 dB/octave gives a net envelope of about −6 dB/octave up to roughly 3 kHz and −12 dB/octave above, before the tube's own filtering. The measured PVC spectrum falls faster than that (about 35 dB between the strongest low harmonics and 1 kHz, close to −12 dB/octave; a high-tongue example, so the region above 1 kHz is also shaped by the player), consistent with Fletcher's remark that the tube's resistance is lower for the upper harmonics than for the resonant fundamental.
- The radiation corner depends on the bell: equating the two asymptotes of the radiation impedance in Fletcher et al. 2006 gives f ≈ 135/d2 (d2 in metres), about 2.7 kHz for a 50 mm end, 1.35 kHz for 100 mm and 0.9 kHz for 150 mm. A wide bell therefore radiates the 1–2 kHz band more efficiently, in line with "flared instruments are louder" and with the players' word "clarity".
- With a 65–80 Hz drone and useful content to about 3 kHz, 40 to 45 harmonics matter; the audible identity sits in harmonics 12–40 (1–3 kHz) and the "weight" in harmonics 1–4.
- A one-pole low-pass (−6 dB/octave) reproduces the idealized net slope but not the measured one on a real tube, where the fall between 300 Hz and 1 kHz is nearer −12 dB/octave, and it cannot make the weakened 2nd–5th harmonics ("hole") that Amir and the UNSW rankings associate with good instruments. A strong narrow peak at the fundamental with a dip just above it is what the measurements describe.
- The two in-mouth figures (130–145 dB and "100 dBA") differ by more than A-weighting explains (it removes roughly 25 dB at 70 Hz, less for the harmonics). The lower figure is the older and less specific one; I would use the 2024 review's value and treat the discrepancy as unresolved.

### Gaps
- No calibrated radiated sound pressure level (dB SPL at a stated distance) or acoustic power of a didjeridu was found. This is a real gap in the accessible literature, not just in my search: the UNSW papers normalise their radiated spectra to the strongest component.
- No published table of harmonic amplitudes for a real termite-made instrument was found; the values above are my readings of two printed figures, one of them a PVC cylinder. Amir 2004, which analyses the spectra of eight real instruments, would be the place to look.
- The press claim that harmonics 2–8 are "gone" is stronger than anything in the peer-reviewed text I could read ("the first few overtones above the fundamental are weak") and should not be used as a number.
- Directivity of the radiated sound was not addressed in any source read.

## 6. What makes an instrument "good"; wall material, wax mouthpiece, bell flare, losses

### Takeaway
Players rank highest the instruments with low input impedance everywhere and above all weak resonances between 1 and 2 kHz (largest peak ≤ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ for the best, ≥ 3 for the worst), because only then can the vocal tract's peaks (a few MPa·s·m⁻³) shape the formants; a strong, sharp first resonance is a secondary plus. Wide bores, flare and rough, lossy walls all push in that direction, and smooth narrow cylinders (PVC) score 2–4 out of 10.

### Cited Findings

The UNSW quality study
- Seven experienced players ranked 38 termite-made instruments and 11 PVC pipes; rankings were compared with impedance spectra measured without a player. "The ranked quality of a didjeridu was found to be negatively correlated with the magnitude of its acoustic input impedance, particularly in the frequency range from 1 to 2 kHz." — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- "The highest ranking instruments have Z ≤ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³, whereas the instruments with lowest ranked quality have Z ≥ 3 MPa·s·m⁻³" (largest value between 1 and 2 kHz; the paper gives p = 5×10⁻⁷ in its Fig. 8 and 3×10⁻⁷ in its Table I). The sensitivity is greatest for the maximum around 1200 Hz and for the rms variation around 1600 Hz; a lower average impedance helps from 200 to 1600 Hz. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Reason: the vocal tract needs impedance peaks "comparable with or greater than" the instrument's to inhibit flow; tract peaks measured at 2–10 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 3 kHz, "a few megohms". — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- No significant correlation of quality with pitch, f1, f2/f1, harmonicity, Z2/Z1, the decay constant λ, the impedance at individual harmonics, or length. Significant: Z0 (negative), Z1 (negative), Z1/Z0 (positive, p = 0.04), Q1 (positive, p = 0.02), sum of the impedance at harmonics 2–11 (negative, p = 0.001), mouth-end diameter (positive, p = 2×10⁻⁴), output diameter (positive, p = 7×10⁻⁶), flare ratio (positive, p = 0.044). — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- "Providing the characteristic input impedance at low frequencies is sufficiently low, players prefer a relatively narrow and strong (i.e., high Q) fundamental resonance." — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Availability of harmonically related resonances in 1–2 kHz ("harmonic coincidence") was negatively correlated with quality: any timbral benefit is "more than countermanded by the necessary associated increase in Z(f)". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- Players' vocabulary and what it tracks: "clarity" (bright and clear against dull, muffled) contributes 67 % of overall quality, "resonance" (fullness) 17 %, loudness 5 %; resonance, loudness, vocals and overtones all correlate negatively with Z0; "speed" correlates only with a higher f1 (r² = 0.51); "backpressure" was the least consistently judged term and, for the shop's assessors, rises with Z0. — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)
- Caussé et al. (2004), as reported: instruments with lower input impedance were also judged better. Amir (2004), as reported: sound recorded from two players on eight instruments, traditional to manufactured; inharmonicity proposed as a positive factor, which the UNSW data did not confirm. — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf); [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- An earlier view by Fletcher: "the main determinant of quality is the smoothness of the walls, on a sub-millimetre scale, and the absence of cavities". — [Fletcher 2003, SMAC paper (hosted by Didjshop)](https://www.didjshop.com/austrAboriginalMusicInstruments.htm); the later measurements favour damped upper resonances instead, see [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)

Plastic pipes and bore diameter
- Nine 38 mm PVC pipes averaged 3.0/10 for overall quality, between the shop's "first class" (4/10) and "second class" (2/10); pipes of 25, 38 and 50 mm at the same pitch scored 2.0, 3.2 and 4.0 out of 10; a 26 mm pipe 2.0/10. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf); [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- For the PVC pipes, the inverse of the cross-sectional area correlates negatively with overall quality, vocals, resonance, loudness and overtones (r² = 0.92, 0.93, 0.59, 0.63, 0.69). — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)
- A plastic pipe "of typical didjeridu dimensions" has several peaks above 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 2 kHz, like the worst termite instrument. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)
- "A good player can produce most of the nuances of traditional performances on a piece of plastic pipe of appropriate diameter and length." — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)

Wall losses, wall material
- Adding felt strips to a PVC bore lowered the peaks above 1 kHz dramatically, but the instruments scored significantly lower: they "were noticeably less loud" and the fundamental resonance was weakened. — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- Four ways shown to lower the 1–2 kHz impedance of a plastic pipe: larger bore, more internal damping and wall loss, tuned Helmholtz resonators along the bore (five, tuned to harmonics 14, 16, 18, 20 and 22), and a flared end section. — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- "Instruments vary … not only in the frequency pattern of higher modes but also in the damping, which is influenced primarily by the smoothness of the tube walls." — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- Maker claims (unmeasured): thin walls "vibrate with the air, absorbing energy", giving a "muffled" sound; walls over 1.0 cm give "crisp, and bright projection"; hardwoods beat softwoods; a smooth varnished bore is "fast, bright, and loud", a rough natural bore "raspy" and "earthy". — [Didgitaldoo design cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)

Bell flare
- Horns tested on a 26 mm pipe: a clarinet bell; a long conical horn flaring from 26 mm to 80 mm over 800 mm ("corresponds to what is found in some didjeridus"); wide horns with an 80 mm throat and 360–420 mm mouth. All reduced the high-frequency impedance peaks; the cone and the large horns made the first resonance weaker than the second; loudness increased with horn size; an experienced player judged the long conical horn best and the large horns "overkill". — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)
- A larger output end "reduces the magnitude of the standing wave and therefore the acoustic impedance associated with resonances" and increases loudness. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

Wax mouthpiece and leaks
- The beeswax (or resinous gum) ring is described only as a seal and comfort aid on the smaller end. — [Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html); [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- A leak near the mouth end lowers the first peak; "small holes make hardly any difference to the amplitude of peaks in the 1–2 kHz range"; a large enough leak makes the drone unplayable. — [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)

### Inferences
- The measurements define a "good" tube as one that behaves like a single strong low resonance followed by an almost featureless, low-impedance response above about 1 kHz. In source-filter terms: the instrument should contribute the body at the fundamental and stay out of the way in the formant band; fixed resonant peaks in 1–2 kHz belong to bad instruments and PVC pipes.
- "Clarity", the players' most important term, goes with low impedance and wide, flared bores, i.e. with better radiation of the upper harmonics and with freedom to place formants; it does not go with a harmonic (cylinder-like) resonance series.
- Material matters through the bore surface (losses) and geometry, as far as the evidence goes: every measured effect in these papers is explained by impedance. I found no measurement isolating wall vibration or wood species, so statements about hardwood, wall thickness or "resonant wood" remain makers' beliefs.
- Fletcher's 2003 "smooth walls" criterion and the 2007–2008 results are best read together: enough smoothness to keep a strong, high-Q fundamental and loudness, enough loss and flare to flatten the peaks above 1 kHz. Felt damping fails because it does the second at the cost of the first.

### Gaps
- No measurement of the acoustic effect of the wax mouthpiece (its opening diameter or shape) was found.
- No controlled comparison of wall materials (eucalypt, bamboo, fibreglass, PVC of equal bore) exists in the sources read; PVC was compared with termite-made wood of different bore only.
- The quality study's instruments are Queensland-made commercial instruments; the authors deliberately did not study instruments of traditional custodians, so nothing here measures what Yolngu players call a good yidaki.

## 7. Attack and decay of the drone; pitch stability and pitch bending

### Takeaway
I found no measurement of the onset or decay time of the drone or of a toot. What is documented is pitch behaviour: the drone is played 2–3 % above the first resonance, the player can move it over a total range of only about a semitone (6 %), usually upward when emphasising formants, and lipping down is easier than lipping up.

### Cited Findings

Transients
- "The steady sound is, however, precisely harmonic, though transients may be influenced by the mode frequencies." — [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)
- "Speed" (how quickly an instrument responds in rhythmic playing, as judged by players) is the one subjective quality tied to the first resonance frequency (r² = 0.51): higher-pitched, shorter instruments are "fast". The authors' explanation: f1 is inversely related to the time sound takes to travel between the ends, so "it seems reasonable that this would also determine how quickly the instrument responds". Amir (2004) is cited for the same observation. — [Smith, Rey, Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)
- The only amplitude-against-time observation in the papers read concerns circular breathing: during each brief inhalation (typically 0.2 s for the player measured) the amplitude is smaller and decreases. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)

Playing pitch relative to the resonance
- "The average difference between the frequency of the nominal playing pitch and f1 was 2 %, with the pitch frequency usually slightly above f1" (38 instruments); one instrument played below f1. — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)
- On the PVC pipe the playing frequency was "close to but slightly above that of the first resonance", about 3 Hz above in one example, as expected for an outward-swinging lip valve. — [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)
- Model value "1.03 times the first tube resonance, which is typical for high-tongue playing" (half a semitone). — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)

Bending range
- The player can shift the lip motion between valve types, "though the total range of variation in the case of the didjeridu is only about a semitone (6 %). Informal observations of competent didjeridu players show that they typically raise the drone frequency when wishing to emphasize formants." — [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)
- "The lip frequency is also flexible: lipping down and to a less extent lipping up are relatively easy on the instrument, as the model of Hollenberg (2000) predicts." — [Wolfe and Smith 2024](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)
- Tongue position moves the pitch as well as the timbre: in the high-tongue / low-tongue sound files "the pitch as well as the timbre is changed by the tongue position". — [UNSW, Physics of the didgeridoo (yidaki)](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidaki.html)
- Hollenberg's simulation (abstract only, as returned by search; full paper not read): with a transverse lip model the sounding frequency rises by up to 10 % when lip closure is significant; with a longitudinal model it hardly changes. — [Hollenberg 2000, Aust. J. Phys. 53, 835](https://publish.csiro.au/ph/PH00100)
- For their analysis the UNSW team assumed a player may vary the drone over "plus or minus one semitone". — [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)

Drift
- Speed of sound c = 331.6 + 0.6·t (m/s, t in °C); a pipe tuned at 20 °C is about 3.4 % sharp at 40 °C, "halfway between C and C#"; the relevant temperature is that of the player's breath in the tube. — [Didjshop, Physics of the didgeridoo](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidj.html) (seller page)
- Added damping (felt) lowered the resonance frequencies and the playing pitch. — [Schneider, Smith, Wolfe 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)

### Inferences
- Time scales implied by the measured numbers, as estimates and not measurements: one trip of sound along a 1.2–1.5 m tube takes 3.5–4.4 ms; one period of the drone is four trips (12–18 ms for 80–55 Hz). The free decay of the first resonance has an amplitude time constant of Q1/(π·f1), about 55 ms for Q1 = 12 and about 390 ms for Q1 = 86 at 70 Hz. In playing the lip end is not a rigid closure, so real ring-down should be at the short end of that range or shorter.
- The build-up of a lip-driven note is governed by the gain of the lip–tube loop, not by the passive Q alone, so these numbers bound the decay better than the attack. The players' "speed" result supports making the response time scale with the period of the drone (shorter, higher instruments respond faster).
- Higher resonances have lower Q and a shorter round trip in terms of their own period, so a toot should start and stop faster than the drone; no source measures this.
- Pitch bend available from the instrument itself is small: about ±0.5 semitone around a centre 0.3–0.5 semitone above the passive resonance. Slides of a tone or more are not part of the acoustic instrument (they belong to slide instruments such as the didjeribone or to vocalisation effects).
- A small upward pitch shift (up to about half a semitone) when the formant is emphasised is documented behaviour, and at harmonics 20–30 it is enough to move a harmonic on or off a bore resonance.

### Gaps
- Attack time, decay time and the shape of the amplitude envelope at note start and stop are not measured in any accessible source, for the drone or for the toot. Amir 2005 ("Articulation in the didjeridu", Forum Acusticum) is the one paper whose title promises such data; I could not obtain it.
- No measurement of pitch drift during a long note or with warming of the tube was found beyond the seller's temperature arithmetic.
- The 10 % figure from Hollenberg comes from a search-result summary of the abstract; it should be checked against the paper before being used.
