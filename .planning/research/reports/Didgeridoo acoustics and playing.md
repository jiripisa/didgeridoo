# The player carves the didgeridoo's sound

Published measurements describe a simple tube and a busy player: the bore supplies one tall, narrow resonance at 50–105 Hz (Q 12–86, 26–37 dB above the tube's general impedance level) and, in the instruments players rank best, almost nothing between 1 and 2 kHz, while lips that stay shut for about half of every cycle and a vocal tract that removes energy do the rest ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf); [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). The tract works by subtraction: its impedance peaks near 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz cut the lip flow by typically 20 dB, and the "formant" the ear follows, near 1.8 kHz and movable over roughly 1–2.2 kHz, is the band left between the notches; with the tongue low there is no formant at all. Against that evidence the weakest blocks of the synthesizer under review (`DidgeEngine`) are the speech vowel table (the first speech formant has at most a weak role, and a second formant below about 1 kHz has no measured counterpart), the additive band-pass pair (peaks on an untouched floor where the instrument makes holes), the sine voice (the real mechanism multiplies two pulse-like valve openings, which keeps the drone's harmonics and adds the voice's own and every m·g ± n·f), and the morph toward a narrow pulse (the measured lip opening has a duty cycle near one half and harmonics falling as n⁻², darker than a sawtooth). The 16-semitone overblow holds up, with second-resonance ratios of 2.6–3.0 (16.5–19 semitones) on 38 measured instruments and 15–17 semitones on a seller's list of 43 Yolŋu yidaki, but traditional players use the toot as a split-second stab, breathe in for about 0.2 s inside the rhythm, and make accents as surges of pressure fired together with a tongue gesture. Prior art offers one worked physical model, the series-impedance calculation of [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf), which its authors call "certainly not quantitatively accurate"; no published real-time physical-model didgeridoo was found, and the commercial imitations are sample libraries. The literature measures neither attack and decay times, nor the toot's spectrum, nor breath noise, nor radiated level, nor vibrato or drift, and the in-mouth data read here come from a few players, two tongue positions and plastic pipes, so those parts of any synthesizer have to be judged by ear against recordings.

*Evidence labels used throughout.* **Measured** is a published measurement. **Authors' interpretation** is the published researchers' own model, explanation or inference. **Observed** is an ethnomusicologist's field description or tempo measurement. **Described** is a player's or teacher's own account, and **claimed** is a statement by a collector, seller, maker, vendor, journalist or encyclopedia; both are unmeasured. **Derived in the notes** is arithmetic, a reading of a printed figure (±5 dB or about ±30 %) or reasoning done by this project's research notes from published numbers: unpublished and unreviewed. The main arithmetic so marked (bore modes, bandwidths, loss figures, notch depths, filter equivalences, interval conversions) was recomputed for this report and agrees with the notes unless stated; the figure readings could not be rechecked, and several equations were restored from damaged PDF text, so compare them with the papers before coding. "Snippet" marks a claim taken from a search-result excerpt, not a full read. Names in backticks are identifiers in `Sources/DidgeridooDSP/DidgeridooEngine.cpp` as read on 2026-11-14.

## The tube gives one sharp resonance at 50–105 Hz, and the best tubes add nothing at 1–2 kHz

### A rough, heavily truncated cone: 1.2–1.5 m long, about 30 mm wide at the lips

**Measured.** A traditional instrument is a termite-hollowed eucalypt stem, "usually about 1.5 metres long" in Arnhem Land and about one metre in Central Australia, with a bore of **about 30 mm at the blowing end and about 50 mm at the free end** and walls of 5–10 mm, "though all these dimensions vary significantly from one instrument to another, even among those by the same maker" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)). The UNSW papers give 1.2–1.5 m as the typical length and a bore "usually somewhat flared from about 30 to 50 mm at the blowing end to about 40 to 150 mm at the open end" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)), with some instruments flaring past 100 mm ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)). The 38 termite-made instruments of the UNSW quality study, made by Indigenous craftsmen near Kuranda in North Queensland and deliberately not instruments of traditional custodians, had mouth ends of 30–60 mm (measured before the wax ring was applied) and **flare ratios of 1.0–2.4** ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html); [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). The shape is "neither a cylinder nor a nearly complete cone" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)): it is "a heavily truncated cone, with a highly irregular surface" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf), Proc. Mtgs. Acoust. 52, dated 2023 in one research note and 2024 in another), rubbed only "to moderate smoothness" and with "enhanced wall losses because of roughness and porosity" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). Unlike brass instruments it has no cup mouthpiece and no constriction; the bore is "relatively wide … along its entire length", comparable in diameter to the player's vocal tract ([Smith, Rey and Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)), which is the geometric reason the tract has so much say. The outside reveals nothing: instruments graded from "high concert" to "second class" look alike, and the bore profiles of the 38 "are not known even to the experimenters" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)).

Fletcher's three Arnhem Land instruments are the only complete set of length, diameters and drone pitch. The first four columns are measured ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)); the last two are derived in the notes by solving his truncated-cone equation (next subsection) with c = 343 m/s and are predictions, since he did not record the second-mode frequencies.

| 159 cm | 31 mm | 36 mm | 60 Hz (B1) | 57 Hz | 2.85 (18.1) |

**Measured, plastic pipes.** Most laboratory numbers in this report come from two "model yidaki": a cylindrical PVC pipe 1210 mm long with a 30 mm bore, and a square plexiglass pipe 1220 mm long and 38 mm wide for optical work; "experienced players reported that both model instruments played moderately well" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). The quality study added 38 mm PVC pipes at pitches from A1 to F2 and pipes of 25, 38 and 50 mm at C2 ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)), and "50 mm appears to be a practical upper limit for players with conventional facial geometry" ([Schneider et al. 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)).

**Claimed or second-hand.** Citing Knopoff 1997, Fletcher and colleagues report that East Arnhem clans (including the Yolŋu), whose style uses higher tube modes for accents, prefer "almost cylindrical didjeridus", while West Arnhem clans prefer "didjeridus with a flaring bore profile" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)); Knopoff's chapter itself was reachable only as a [bibliographic record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/acde1b09-34e3-4447-8ab0-ac11b67f362c). A specialist seller's archive of 43 sold yidaki by named Yolŋu makers lists lengths of 114–170 cm (mostly 130–157 cm), mouthpieces of 2.7–4.1 cm (most often 3.2 cm) and "bells" of 7.6–14 cm with one of 23 cm, without saying whether the bell figure is an inside diameter ([Ginger Root Rhythms](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm)). A collector's database gives 120–145 cm for north-east Arnhem instruments and 105–132 cm for West Arnhem mago ([iDIDJ database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)). Wikipedia gives 1–3 m with most around 1.2 m, calls the West Arnhem mako "usually shorter", and lists modern materials from bamboo to carbon fibre with bells of 5–20 cm ([Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)). A maker's unmeasured rules of thumb are a 2.8–3.2 cm mouthpiece, a narrow first 30 cm, a bell of 8–15 cm and walls thicker than 1 cm ([Didgitaldoo cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)); the same page contradicts itself on whether a cone must be longer or shorter than a cylinder for the same note, and the notes did not use it for pitch.

**Derived in the notes.** A cylinder (PVC, bamboo) is the edge of this family, not its centre: a flare ratio of 1.0 occurred in the measured set, but most instruments flare. The Knopoff statement and the seller's data sit uneasily together, because wide bells and toot intervals well below a twelfth require real taper. A narrow, near-cylindrical upper section with a flare lower down would reconcile them, but no measured bore profile of a traditional yidaki was found.

### The drone sits at 55–80 Hz, up to six semitones above what length alone predicts

**Measured.** The drone lies "in the frequency range 55 to 80 Hz", "usually about 70 Hz" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)); the lowest resonance "is typically 50 to 80 Hz" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). The 38 instruments had nominal playing pitches from **A1 (55 Hz) to G2 (98 Hz)** and first resonances of about 50–105 Hz, distributed as five each at F2, E2 and C2, four each at D#2 and D2, three each at G2, C#2, B1 and A#1 and two at A1 (the printed counts add up to 37), and pitch showed no significant correlation with ranked quality ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). Players "seem to prefer instruments in the pitch range approximately A1 to F2", the middle of which suits the heterodyne voice effects of typical male voices ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)). "Traditional makers and players seem to have little concern with either the drone frequency or the interval to the second mode" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)).

**Claimed.** A large shop lists the twelve keys from A up to G# as the commonest ([Didjshop, musical keys](https://www.didjshop.com/shop1/didj_musical_keys.php)). The notes' tally of the Ginger Root archive gives E ×9, D ×9, F ×7, Eb ×7, F# ×4, C# ×2, C ×2, Ab ×1 and two between keys ([Ginger Root Rhythms](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm)), and the collector's database gives fundamentals "mainly D to F#" for north-east Arnhem Land ([iDIDJ database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)). A forum copy of Knopoff's Grove article (snippet) puts the Arnhem Land fundamental between B and G ([francedidgeridoo.com](https://francedidgeridoo.com/download/file.php?id=5581)). Sources disagree on octave names (Wikipedia writes A2–G3, UNSW A1–F2), so trust the hertz values.

**Authors' model.** A cylinder closed at the lips resonates at f_n = (n − ½)·c/2L′, that is c/4L′, 3c/4L′, 5c/4L′, with acoustic length L′ = L + 0.3·d2. A truncated cone with diameter d1 at the lips and d2 at the open end has impedance maxima at k_n·L′ = nπ − arctan(k_n·x1), where x1 = d1·L/(d2 − d1) is the distance from the cone's apex to the blowing end and k_n = 2πf_n/c ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html); [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). For small flare the 2006 paper gives ω_n ≈ (n − ½)·(πc/2L′)·{1 + [1 + 4(d2 − d1)/(π²·d1·(n − ½)²)]^½}, which returns 1, 3, 5 × c/4L′ for a cylinder and 2, 4, 6 × c/4L′ for a complete cone, with "a stretched quasiharmonic sequence" in between; the notes restored this formula from damaged text. Flare raised the fundamental of Fletcher's three instruments "by a factor between about 1.06 and 1.38 relative to a cylindrical tube of the same length": his second instrument "is only 3 percent shorter than the third, but its fundamental frequency is 25 percent higher because of its large flare" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)).

**Claimed and amateur.** Makers size PVC by f = c/4L with c = 344 m/s and no end correction: 1.315 m for C (65.40 Hz), and 81.90 Hz from 1.05 m ([Didjshop, Physics of the didgeridoo](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidj.html)). A student paper measured about 63 Hz on a 1.60 m termite instrument whose cylinder value is 53.75 Hz ([Hammond, The Physics of Dreamtime](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidjDreamtime.html)), 17 % sharp and inside Fletcher's range.

**Derived in the notes.** The cone equation predicts 57, 80 and 64 Hz for Fletcher's instruments against the measured 60, 80 and 64 Hz, so **three plain numbers (L, d1, d2) fix the drone to within a semitone**, while length alone falls short by one to nearly six semitones. The small-flare formula gives modes at 1.17, 3.07, 5.04 and 7.03 × c/4L′ (ratios 1 : 2.61 : 4.30 : 5.99) for (d2 − d1)/d1 = 0.5, and 1.31, 3.13, 5.08 and 7.06 (1 : 2.39 : 3.88 : 5.39) for 1.0; it loses accuracy for the first mode as flare grows. Equal-tempered values for the keys in question are A1 55.0, A#1 58.3, B1 61.7, C2 65.4, C#2 69.3, D2 73.4, D#2 77.8, E2 82.4, F2 87.3, F#2 92.5 and G2 98.0 Hz. The Queensland set spreads evenly over C2–F2 and the Yolŋu list clusters on D to F, so a default drone between C2 and E2 is central for both. In the engine, where a key sounds two octaves below its MIDI note at `octave` 0, the measured A1–G2 range corresponds to MIDI notes 57–67.

### The first impedance peak stands 26–37 dB above the tube's general level

**Measured** on the 38 termite-made instruments with an impedance head at the mouth end, 50–3000 Hz at 1.346 Hz resolution ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). Impedances are in MPa·s·m⁻³; f1, f2 are the first and second maxima, Z1, Z2 their magnitudes, Z0 the characteristic impedance (geometric mean of the first maximum and first minimum), Q1 the quality factor of the first peak.

| Quantity | Measured range | Relation to ranked quality |
|---|---|---|
| f1 | about 50–105 Hz | none |
| Z0 | about 0.2–1 | lower is better (p = 5×10⁻⁶) |
| Z1 | about 5–40 | lower is better (p = 1×10⁻⁴) |
| Z1/Z0 | about 20–70 | higher is better (p = 0.04) |
| Q1 | 12–86 | higher is better (p = 0.02) |
| f2/f1 | about 2.6–3.0 | none |
| Harmonicity (slope of f_n against (2n − 1)·f1, first six peaks) | 0.72–1.0 (1 = cylinder) | none |
| Z2/Z1 | about 0.2–1.4 | none |
| λ in Z_i = Z1·exp[−λ(f_i − f1)], first 3–6 peaks | 1–6 kHz⁻¹ | none |
| Largest peak between 1 and 2 kHz | ≤ 1 for the best, ≥ 3 for the worst | strongest predictor (p = 3×10⁻⁷) |

The less formal UNSW account of the same data puts the first peak, "with one exception", between 8 and 30 ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)). "In general, the magnitudes of the first few peaks in the spectrum decrease monotonically with increasing frequency"; only one of the 38 had Z2 significantly above Z1. Z0 scaled with the inverse square of the mouth diameter at a fitted slope of 190 ± 30 against about 500 for a cylinder, roughly 0.4 of the impedance of a cylinder with the same mouth ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). The wide bore makes the characteristic impedance "lower by an order of magnitude than that of most of the members of the lip-driven musical instrument family", and the smooth 30 mm PVC pipe still shows peaks of 3–10 around 1.5 kHz, the same size as a raised-tongue vocal tract ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Felt strips inside a PVC pipe lowered the resonance frequencies and the playing pitch and had "a dramatic effect on Z(f) above 1 kHz"; large horns left a "nearly complete lack of resonances for wavelengths less than about 0.5 m", which is everything above roughly 690 Hz ([Schneider et al. 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)).

**Authors' model.** The characteristic impedance of a tube of diameter d is Z0 = 4ρc/πd². Wall attenuation is α ≈ 10⁻⁵·β·ω^½/d in SI units, with β "about 2.4 for perfectly smooth walls" and "much larger" for a rough natural bore; the authors' own model tube (1.3 m, 40 mm) uses β = 12. "In a widely flaring tube, overtones other than the first are rather difficult to produce because of the rapidly decreasing quality factor of the tube resonances" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). Flare compresses all the mode intervals, and "for moderate flare, only the lowest mode frequency is significantly affected" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)); a horn on the end likewise "contract[s]" the ratios because its effective length grows with frequency ([Schneider et al. 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)). Brass instruments have "a dozen or so impedance maxima … nearly all falling close to a harmonic series", whereas the didjeridu's harmonics "need only occasionally and 'accidentally' coincide with the impedance peaks" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)).

**Derived in the notes.** The −3 dB width of the first resonance is f1/Q1, **about 0.8–6 Hz for a 70 Hz drone**, and Z1/Z0 of 20–70 is 26–37 dB. The decay law λ = 1–6 kHz⁻¹ equals about 9 dB per kHz in the most resonant instruments and 52 dB per kHz in the most damped, and the good instruments are at the damped end. Neighbouring maxima lie about c/2L apart, 115–145 Hz for a 1.2–1.5 m tube, so 7–9 narrow bore resonances fall between 1 and 2 kHz; nothing in the measurements suggests a broad formant-like band belonging to the tube itself. Fletcher's α formula gives a wall-loss Q of about 51 at 70 Hz for a smooth 40 mm tube (β = 2.4) and about 10 for β = 12, which brackets most of the measured 12–86, and a round-trip amplitude gain exp(−2αL) for the 1.3 m model tube of 0.85 at 70 Hz, 0.65 at 500 Hz, 0.47 at 1.5 kHz and 0.34 at 3 kHz for β = 12, against 0.97, 0.92, 0.86 and 0.81 for β = 2.4. The notes also read the harmonicity slope as a plain multiplier, which would put the second peak at 2.2–3.0·f1; the lower end disagrees with the measured f2/f1 of 2.6–3.0, so that reading is unsafe and the measured ratio and the cone equation are the numbers to use.

### Flared tubes weaken harmonics 2 to 5; only cylinders favour the odd ones

**Measured.** On the cylindrical PVC pipe, played about 3 Hz above its first resonance at about 70 Hz, odd harmonics dominate the radiated sound at low frequencies; approaching 1 kHz "the odd-even difference in the sound spectrum disappears, because in this frequency range the harmonics fall almost midway between resonances"; around 1.5 kHz "it is the even harmonics that benefit", and in a low-tongue example odd harmonics are raised again around 2.5 kHz ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Inside the mouth the harmonic amplitudes "fall almost monotonically", while the radiated sound of a cylinder "has relatively weak second, fourth, and sixth harmonics" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)); the lip motion itself shows no odd-even bias, so the pattern belongs to the bore ([Wolfe and Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)). A second resonance close to 3·f1 pulls on the pitch: the one instrument with Z2 above Z1 was played at 98 Hz, between f1 = 102 Hz and f2/3 = 92 Hz ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)).

**Authors' interpretation.** "The sound spectrum of the didjeridu … is strictly harmonic. The fact that the upper modes of the pipe are not in harmonic relation to the fundamental affects only the strength of certain harmonics" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)); "transients may be influenced by the mode frequencies" ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)). The flow-to-output transfer function "has maxima at the same frequencies as does the input impedance at the lip end of the tube", so a harmonic that lands on an impedance peak radiates strongly. In the model cylinder played at 1.03·f1, "an actual transfer of emphasis [from odd to even harmonics] takes place at about 1000 Hz and another one in the reverse direction at about 3500 Hz". With a flared bore "there are no clear frequency regions in which even or odd harmonics dominates. In addition, the low harmonics above the fundamental are generally weaker because their frequencies do not approximate those of tube resonances" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). Amir, as reported by the UNSW group, found a characteristic "hole" in the spectrum, "the first few overtones above the fundamental are weak" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)), and his abstract states that "the random nature of the instrument bore is actually conducive to creating its typical timbre" ([Amir 2004, abstract](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/1170716.htm)). A press account sharpens this to "the second to eighth harmonic overtones are gone" ([Ingeniøren 2004](https://ing.dk/artikel/termitter-giver-den-australske-didjeridoo-dens-fantastiske-lyd)); that is stronger than anything in the peer-reviewed text read and is not a usable number. Because the 1–2 kHz band holds the 20th to 30th harmonics, "changes in the playing frequency of only a couple of percent (a fraction of a semitone) can produce or remove" the coincidence of a harmonic with a bore resonance; this "harmonic coincidence" yields "broad and weak" formants, smaller than those the vocal tract makes ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)).

**Amateur.** One spectrum of a 1.60 m termite instrument shows overtones "in decreasing intensity" plus a component near 300 Hz that is "the loudest component besides the drone fundamental" in every tone, and a raised one near 170 Hz, both unaffected by mouth shape ([Hammond, The Physics of Dreamtime](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidjDreamtime.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** Strong odd-harmonic dominance below about 1 kHz is the signature of a cylinder (PVC, bamboo), not of a termite-made instrument, whose low spectrum is unpatterned with a strong fundamental and weakened second to about fifth harmonics. The toot interval and the drone's third harmonic are linked by the bore: an instrument whose toot is a twelfth is the one with a strong third harmonic, and one whose toot is a tenth has its second resonance near 2.4–2.5·f1, between the second and third harmonics, helping neither.

### Players rank instruments by how little they resonate at 1–2 kHz

**Measured.** Seven experienced players ranked the 38 termite-made instruments and 11 PVC pipes, and the rankings were compared with impedance spectra taken without a player. "The ranked quality of a didjeridu was found to be negatively correlated with the magnitude of its acoustic input impedance, particularly in the frequency range from 1 to 2 kHz": **the highest-ranked instruments have Z ≤ 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ there and the lowest-ranked Z ≥ 3** (the paper prints p = 5×10⁻⁷ in its Fig. 8 and 3×10⁻⁷ in its Table I). The sensitivity is greatest for the maximum around 1200 Hz and for the rms variation around 1600 Hz, and a lower average impedance helps from 200 to 1600 Hz. Quality showed no significant correlation with pitch, f1, f2/f1, harmonicity, Z2/Z1, λ, the impedance at individual harmonics or length; it correlated negatively with Z0, Z1 and the summed impedance at harmonics 2–11 (p = 0.001), and positively with Z1/Z0, Q1, mouth-end diameter (p = 2×10⁻⁴), output diameter (p = 7×10⁻⁶) and flare ratio (p = 0.044). The availability of harmonically placed resonances at 1–2 kHz correlated negatively with quality ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). In the players' vocabulary, "clarity" contributes 67 % of overall quality, "resonance" 17 % and loudness 5 %; resonance, loudness, "vocals" and "overtones" all correlate negatively with Z0; "speed" correlates only with a higher f1 (r² = 0.51); "backpressure" was the least consistently judged term ([Smith, Rey and Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)).

Plastic pipes score low. Nine 38 mm PVC pipes averaged 3.0 out of 10, between the shop's "first class" (4) and "second class" (2); pipes of 25, 38 and 50 mm at one pitch scored 2.0, 3.2 and 4.0 ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)), and for PVC the inverse of the cross-sectional area correlates negatively with overall quality and "vocals" at r² = 0.92 and 0.93 ([Smith, Rey and Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)). A plastic pipe "of typical didjeridu dimensions" has several peaks above 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 2 kHz, like the worst termite instrument ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)). Four changes lower that band on a pipe: a larger bore, more wall loss, tuned Helmholtz resonators along the bore (five, at harmonics 14, 16, 18, 20 and 22) and a flared end. Felt damping flattened the peaks above 1 kHz but scored worse, because the pipes "were noticeably less loud" and the fundamental resonance weakened. Of the horns tried on a 26 mm pipe, a long cone flaring from 26 to 80 mm over 800 mm, which "corresponds to what is found in some didjeridus", was judged best; horns with 360–420 mm mouths were "overkill", and loudness rose with horn size ([Schneider et al. 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)). A leak near the mouth end lowers the first peak, while "small holes make hardly any difference to the amplitude of peaks in the 1–2 kHz range" ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)). The beeswax ring is described only as a seal and comfort aid ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)).

**Authors' interpretation.** The vocal tract can inhibit flow only where its impedance peaks are "comparable with or greater than" the instrument's, and tract peaks measure 2–10 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 3 kHz, so a quiet 1–2 kHz band in the tube hands that band to the player ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). "Providing the characteristic input impedance at low frequencies is sufficiently low, players prefer a relatively narrow and strong (i.e., high Q) fundamental resonance", and any timbral benefit of harmonic coincidence is "more than countermanded by the necessary associated increase in Z(f)" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)). The rankings suggest players "preferred a dominant fundamental in the sound and weak second and other low harmonics", which leaves the moving formants more prominent, and the authors explain "speed" by the travel time of sound along a shorter tube ([Smith, Rey and Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)). Caussé and colleagues also found lower-impedance instruments judged better, while Amir's proposal that inharmonicity is a positive factor was not confirmed by the UNSW data ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)). Fletcher's earlier view that "the main determinant of quality is the smoothness of the walls, on a sub-millimetre scale, and the absence of cavities" ([Fletcher 2003, hosted by Didjshop](https://www.didjshop.com/austrAboriginalMusicInstruments.htm)) predates these measurements, though he had also written that "a good player can produce most of the nuances of traditional performances on a piece of plastic pipe of appropriate diameter and length" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)).

**Claimed.** Makers hold that thin walls "vibrate with the air, absorbing energy", that walls over 1 cm give "crisp, and bright projection", that hardwoods beat softwoods and that a varnished bore is "fast, bright, and loud" while a natural one is "raspy" ([Didgitaldoo cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)). No measurement isolating wall vibration or wood species was found; every measured effect in the papers is explained by bore impedance.

**Derived in the notes.** In source-filter terms a good tube is a single strong low resonance followed by an almost featureless, low-impedance response above about 1 kHz. Fixed resonant peaks at 1–2 kHz are the mark of bad instruments and plastic pipes. Fletcher's "smooth walls" and the later results fit together as: smooth enough to keep a strong, high-Q fundamental and loudness, lossy and flared enough to flatten everything above 1 kHz.

### About forty harmonics radiate, and the ear follows the ones at 1–2 kHz

**Authors' model.** With a sinusoidal lip opening that just closes once per cycle, the flow waveform "gives a spectral envelope which is initially nearly constant and then declines at about 12 dB/octave", and "the transfer function between flow spectrum and acoustic radiation rises at 6 dB/octave at low frequencies and is then flat above about 3 kHz for the didjeridu horn" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)). The radiation impedance of the open end is about 7×10⁻⁴·ω² + 0.5j·ρω/d2 at low frequency and tends to ρc/A2 ≈ 500/d2² at high frequency (SI units, restored from damaged text), and the radiated power of each harmonic is the radiation resistance times the squared flow at the open end ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)).

**Measured.** The radiated sound of the PVC pipe, taken on axis 125 mm from the end during a high-tongue drone at about 70 Hz, shows strong harmonics with the odd ones dominant at the bottom, formants at about 1.5–2 kHz, and minima at the frequencies of the tract's impedance maxima ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). On real instruments Fletcher recorded "a pronounced formant band at about 1500 Hz", moved to "about 2.2 kHz" by constricting the mouth, with "some evidence for a lower vocal-tract formant at about 500 Hz" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)). Sound pressure inside the player's mouth at a comfortable level is **about 130–145 dB re 20 µPa**, with pressure waves "with amplitudes around one kPa" ([Wolfe and Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf); [Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)); older UNSW pages say the level in the mouth "can be 100 dBA" ([UNSW, yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidaki.html); [UNSW, yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)). "Highly flared instruments are usually louder" ([Smith, Rey and Wolfe 2011](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2011didjeridu.pdf)).

**Authors' interpretation.** "Human hearing sensitivity declines rapidly below about 300 Hz. Consequently, despite their relatively large amplitude, the fundamental and lower harmonics are not very loud. Further, they vary little during playing. The formants, on the other hand, occur at frequencies in the range of maximum sensitivity of the ear" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). "The formants that give the didjeridu its characteristic sound fall in the range roughly 1–2 kHz", which "corresponds roughly to the 20th to 30th harmonic of the normal drone note" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)). Amir's quality criterion, as reported, rests on "the levels of harmonics 2 to 5 relative to that of the fundamental", low levels marking an excellent instrument ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)).

**Derived in the notes (figure readings).** The table gives approximate levels read from Fig. 4(c) of Tarnopolsky et al. for the PVC cylinder with the tongue high, relative to the strongest component. On those readings the formant stands 15–25 dB above the notches on either side and at about the level the envelope had already reached by 1 kHz; this report notes that a subtractive mechanism predicts exactly that.

A reading of Fig. 1 of [Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf) gives, for an "uninflected drone", low harmonics 30–35 dB above the level reached beyond 1.5–2 kHz, and for a "nasal drone" a formant band at about 2–3 kHz whose strongest harmonics rise to about the level of the low ones. Combining Fletcher's two slopes gives a first-order radiated envelope of about −6 dB per octave up to roughly 3 kHz and −12 dB per octave above, before the tube's own filtering; the measured cylinder falls faster, about 35 dB between the strongest low harmonics and 1 kHz, close to −12 dB per octave. Equating the two asymptotes of the radiation impedance puts the corner at f ≈ 135/d2 (d2 in metres): about 2.7 kHz for a 50 mm end, 1.35 kHz for 100 mm and 0.9 kHz for 150 mm, so a wide bell radiates the formant band more efficiently. With a 65–80 Hz drone and useful content to about 3 kHz, 40 to 45 harmonics matter: the identity sits in harmonics 12–40 and the weight in harmonics 1–4. The two in-mouth figures differ by more than A-weighting explains (about 25 dB at 70 Hz), and the notes treat the discrepancy as unresolved in favour of the newer value.

## The player supplies the rest: lips shut half the cycle and a tract that cuts 20 dB notches

### The lip valve makes a rounded half-cycle pulse whose harmonics fall as n⁻²

**Measured.** High-speed video at 1000 frames per second and stroboscopic schlieren images of a player on the square plexiglass pipe show that "the lips are closed for about half of each cycle, thus generating high levels of upper harmonics of the lip frequency"; the lip period was 14.3 ms, about 70 Hz. The motion "conforms to the 'outward swinging door model'", opening under excess upstream pressure, and the playing frequency lies slightly above the first pipe resonance as that valve type requires ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Earlier stroboscopy contrasted this with a trumpet player's lips, "which normally have a nearly sinusoidal motion and just touch for a very small fraction of each cycle" ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)). The opening is "typically nearly elliptical", and the harmonic amplitudes of the opening area, averaged over ten cycles, follow **s_n ∝ n⁻² "to a reasonable approximation"**; with the tongue low the harmonics from about the fifth upward fall "even more rapidly than n⁻²" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). The camera "limits the maximum frequency in an experimental spectrum A(f) to 500 Hz", about seven harmonics, so the law is unverified above that. Just after opening the jet is narrow and well defined; it broadens, deviates downward and mixes turbulently with the air in the tube. **Mouth pressure is 0.8–2 kPa and flow 0.1–0.3 L/s** over a range of loudness ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)); Fletcher's earlier figures are "about 1–2 kPa (10–20 cm water gauge pressure) for the drone note and about 4–5 kPa to produce the second mode" ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). The lips radiate "roughly as loudly" backwards into the mouth as into the instrument ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)).

**Authors' model.** The idealised area function is S(t) = A·⟨cos ωt − cos φ⟩ with negative values set to zero, where φ/π is the open fraction, "typically about half of the period for didjeridu players, φ ≈ π/2, though this may vary somewhat with playing style". The authors ascribe the difference between this "transparent closure" model and the measurement to "a 'soft' closure of the lips instead of the 'sharp' closure", and suggest the player "may be able to modify this relation to some extent by changing the tension in the lip muscles". Flow is not area: U = [2(p_mouth − p_instrument)/ρ]^½·S(t), and the tract and instrument impedances act in series on the valve ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). The older model assumed a sinusoidal opening that just closes, an assumption the half-cycle closure later contradicted ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). Hollenberg's time-domain simulations, read as an abstract only, find that with the "approximately half-closed cycle" a transverse lip model sounds below and a longitudinal (swinging) model above both the lip and the pipe resonance, that closure raises the transverse model's frequency "by up to 10%" and leaves the swinging model "essentially unaltered", and that lips sticking on closure "weaken significantly the generation of harmonics" ([Hollenberg 2000, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1071/PH00100); also at [CSIRO Publishing](https://www.publish.csiro.au/PH/PH00100) and [publish.csiro.au/ph/PH00100](https://publish.csiro.au/ph/PH00100)).

**Derived in the notes.** A sawtooth has harmonics falling as 1/n (−6 dB per octave); the measured lip area falls as 1/n² (−12 dB per octave) at a duty cycle near 50 %. The real valve is therefore about 6 dB per octave darker than a sawtooth, and a morph toward a narrow pulse moves away from the measurement; a half-wave-rectified cosine with softened corners is the closest simple shape. Without a separate radiation stage, the +6 dB per octave radiation transfer below about 3 kHz turns the −12 dB per octave flow into a −6 dB per octave radiated envelope, which is the sawtooth's slope: a sawtooth can be right for the radiated sound and wrong for the flow, and the two must not be counted twice. "Loudness" and "lip tension" most plausibly map to how soft the closure is and to small changes of the open fraction around one half, not to a pulse-width sweep; this rests on two remarks in the 2006 paper, not on a measured series. At 1 kPa the jet speed is about 41 m/s, so the measured flow corresponds to a time-averaged opening of only 2.5–7 mm², hard to reconcile with the model's 50 mm² (labelled "maximum area" in the paper's table and used as a mean in its formula); treat both as orders of magnitude. The acoustic pressure in the mouth, about 1 kPa in amplitude, is as large as the blowing pressure, so the valve works in a strongly non-linear regime and a linear source-filter model is an approximation from the start. A caution added by this report: one note records the paper as saying the idealised model predicts n⁻¹, but a clipped cosine has only slope kinks, which give n⁻² asymptotically, so check that sentence against the PDF.

### The vocal tract removes energy at 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz, and the formant is what remains

**Measured.** An impedance probe just inside the lips during playing (0.2–3.0 kHz in 5.383 Hz steps) on the PVC pipe, with three players, shows that with the tongue high "the vocal tract impedance shows broad peaks at approximately 1.5, 2.1, and 2.8 kHz"; the 1.5 kHz peak is "about 8 MPa s m⁻³", and across the study the tract maxima are **2–10 MPa·s·m⁻³ between 1 and 3 kHz**, "comparable with or larger than a typical impedance maximum of the yidaki in that frequency range". The result is "a strong formant between about 1.5 and 2 kHz, whose frequency and amplitude may be varied by the performer", near 1.7 kHz in the published figures. Envelope extrema between 1.0 and 2.2 kHz plotted against the nearest impedance extrema give a slope of 0.93 and a correlation coefficient of 0.98: "the impedance maxima correspond almost exactly to minima in the spectral envelope of the sound, while maxima in the sound spectrum occur on average at frequencies slightly above those of the minimum in the impedance". With the tongue low the peaks are "not as large", there is "none of the shaping of the spectral envelope" and "no clear formant comparable to those near 1.7 kHz". No clear impedance maxima appeared for the traditional player, who found the probe disruptive ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)); the correlation rests on "46 measurements on three players" ([UNSW, yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidaki.html)). Tract resonances are "much broader than the frequency differences between the harmonics of the lip vibration", so "several harmonics of the played sound will fall within a formant". Broadband sound injected into the mouth and leaking through the lips comes out with "an envelope that resembles the inverse of the vocal tract impedance" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)).

**Authors' interpretation.** Sufficiently high tract peaks "inhibit acoustic airflow entering the instrument at those frequencies, by typically 20 dB", the authors' own reading of their Figs. 4 and 6 ([Smith et al. 2007, web copy](https://www.didjshop.com/physicsVocalTractResonances.html)). In the group's words the peaks "coincide with antiformants at about 1.5 and 2.0 kHz, leaving a prominent formant … at about 1.8 kHz"; "peaks in |Z_tract| create antiformants and minima in |Z_tract| correspond to formants" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)). The radiated formants "correspond approximately to those of the second formant frequency produced in speech with the same tract configuration", "comparable with, but not equal to" F2 because the lips are almost shut; the first speech formant, "below about 1 kHz for all vowels", is not the one involved, and "some vowel shapes with low tongue may not produce a clear formant" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Speech from the same lab makes the point concrete: tracts saying "heed" have a strong resonance at about 1.8 kHz, and "hoard" or "hot" have none between 1 and 2 kHz ([UNSW, yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)). The theory paper models the tract as two series resonances (impedance minima) at 1.5 and 2.5 kHz with Q = 10 and a characteristic impedance of 4 MPa·s·m⁻³, plus a glottis and lung branch, and obtains "two pronounced formants … their center frequencies coincide with those of the two minima in the vocal-tract impedance" and "a pronounced minimum at the frequency of the maximum in the tract impedance" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)).

Two earlier accounts are superseded in detail. Fletcher's 1996 picture was the mouth as a heavily damped Helmholtz resonator, tunable "from about 500 Hz to about 3 kHz", whose bandwidth "encompasses the frequencies of several harmonics" that it enhances ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). The 2001 conference paper, working from mimed playing, found tract maxima "in the range 1.5–2.5 kHz" for the constricted sound and associated formants with impedance maxima ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)); the 2006 measurement during playing shows the opposite. A weak tract peak below 500 Hz often appears when players mime but not during playing, and in one recording a tract resonance strongly emphasises the fifth harmonic of the drone ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf); [UNSW, yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** Linearising the paper's flow equation gives, for harmonic n, a flow equal to the unloaded flow divided by (1 + Z_n/R_lip), where Z_n is the tract plus bore impedance at that frequency and R_lip = (2ρp0)^½/s0. With the paper's p0 = 1 kPa and s0 = 50 mm², **R_lip is about 1 MPa·s·m⁻³**, and a resistive peak of 2, 4, 8 or 10 MPa·s·m⁻³ cuts the flow by about 10, 14, 19 or 21 dB; a tract minimum of 0.4 costs only about 3 dB. That reproduces the reported 20 dB for the measured 8 MPa·s·m⁻³ peak and explains why tubes below 1 MPa·s·m⁻³ are preferred. Because the load is passive, the tract can only attenuate: a formant is the band of least attenuation, never a boost above the unloaded source, which is the essential difference from speech. The measured notch centres are 600–700 Hz apart, so the pass band holds roughly 7–9 harmonics of a 70 Hz drone; the model's Q of 10 means resonance widths of 150 Hz at 1.5 kHz and 250 Hz at 2.5 kHz. Against a five-point speech table with F1 at 300–700 Hz and F2 at 650–2250 Hz, the documented and controllable band is F2-like and lives at about 1–2.2 kHz, with the next one near 3 kHz; the F1 band is at best weak; the measured counterpart of the open vowels is the absence of a formant, not an F2 gliding down to 650–1100 Hz; and no measurement exists for an "oo" shape during playing.

### Only two tongue shapes have numbers, and a nearly closed glottis is what makes formants strong

**Measured.** "High tongue" brings the tongue close to the hard palate and is "very common in yidaki performance"; "low tongue" has "no lingual constriction" and gives "a sound without a strong formant … used as a contrast to the high-tongue drone" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). MRI during playing shows the tract "open" and of roughly uniform width from lips to vocal folds for the uninflected drone, and constricted between the lips and the soft palate for the "nasal" sound ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)). Reducing the mouth volume raised the band from about 1.5 kHz to about 2.2 kHz ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). An artificial player with a controllable "glottis" shows weaker resonances and weaker formants when it is open ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)). With the folds in the whisper position and no intent to sing, the sound in the mouth drives the vocal folds at the drone frequency with an amplitude comparable to deliberate singing ([Wolfe and Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)). MRI confirms an interdental articulation, the tongue "thrust forward between the teeth and allowed to vibrate against the lower lip" and then retracted, "creating a distinct pulse in the sound" ([Wiggins and Storey 2010](https://cds.ismrm.org/protected/10MProceedings/PDFfiles/5007_7323.PDF)). Ultrasound of one player with three years' experience documents five articulations without acoustic analysis: a cyclic "undulation" of the tongue body from the alveolar ridge back to the velum, a "piston" (tongue retracted, then shot forward, a high-amplitude burst), an alveolar trill that "can sound like a growl, and this is often what it is called", and alveolar and velar ejectives ([Zolotas and Bird 2010](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/view/2313)).

**Authors' model.** In the high-tongue position the tongue forms "a narrow passage of effective diameter about 10 mm from the root of the tongue up to the lips"; in the low-tongue position the mouth "simply extends the trachea into a uniform tube up to the lips", about 20 mm in diameter. An adducted glottis, about 2 × 10 mm (20 mm²) and 5 mm thick, has an inertive impedance of about 2–6 MPa·s·m⁻³ at 1–3 kHz against about 1 for the trachea and about 2 (resistive) for the lungs, so it shields the tract resonances from lung damping ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). The useful glottis position is "something like the position used for whispering", and learning to play with the glottis nearly closed "may be important in developing technique" ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html); [Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). The UNSW page describes alternating the two shapes as "a little like someone alternating between the sound 'ee' … and an indistinct vowel something like 'aw'", and notes that "the pitch as well as the timbre is changed by the tongue position" ([UNSW, yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** A vowel control is better treated as two quantities: where the constriction sits (band frequency, about 1.5–2.2 kHz) and how tight it is together with how closed the glottis is (band depth, zero to about 20 dB). The "undulation" is a constriction travelling from front to back and is the nearest documented gesture to a vowel glide, but no frequencies are published for it. "Growl" is ambiguous among players: it can mean a tongue-tip trill as well as voice.

### The voice multiplies the lip flow; a fifth or a tenth above the drone gives f/2

**Measured.** With drone f = 70 Hz and voice g = 3f/2 = 105 Hz, every component of the sound is equally spaced at f/2 = 35 Hz ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). An electrode study of one non-expert player on plastic pipes recorded f ≈ 71 Hz with g ≈ 106 Hz (a fifth), f ≈ 155 Hz with g ≈ 206 Hz (a fourth) and f ≈ 164 Hz with g ≈ 205 Hz (a major third), with difference tones at f/2, f/3 and f/4; "the difference frequency g − f itself is rather weak in the sound signal, in part because lower frequencies are less well radiated". The heterodyne components appear in the lip motion and the vocal-fold motion as well as in the sound, and the lips' influence on the folds is "much stronger than the reverse". Singing "oo" to match the pipe's output gave 136–140 dB in the mouth and humming about 140–145 dB, against 130–145 dB from the lips alone; a vocal third above a 71 Hz drone was too low to sing strongly ([Wolfe and Smith 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/WolfeSmithAA.pdf)). A voice close to the drone pitch gives "very strong 'beats'" until the player locks it to the lips, and when the voice glides "the heterodyne frequencies also glide, some in either direction" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)).

**Authors' model.** Lips and vocal folds are two valves in series on one air stream; both "'clip' the total current when they close", so flow is proportional to the product of the two opening areas, U = A·⟨sin ω_D·t − x1⟩·⟨sin ω_V·t − x2⟩ with negative values dropped, and contains "components at all frequencies nω₁ ± mω₂", strongest for small m and n. Players sing "a musical fifth (frequency ratio 3:2) or more usually a musical tenth (frequency ratio 5:2) above the drone fundamental", which "generates an impressive subharmonic drone of frequency ω_D/2, together with all its harmonics" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). For the tenth, "there is not much radiated energy in this sub-octave fundamental, but the subjective pitch is generated strongly from the sequence of harmonics"; animal calls use a voice pitch "rather high so that frequency mixing does not have such a pronounced effect" ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). "Vocalisations are much more common in modern styles than in traditional performance", and the very low tone "can be quite loud and impressive" ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** Ring-modulating the lip source with a sine at g produces only n·f ± g (the notes worked from a description of the engine as a ring modulator; its code keeps a carrier, as the block table below records). The physical product is of two openings that never go negative, each with a steady part and many harmonics; it keeps the drone harmonics, adds the voice harmonics m·g, and adds every m·g ± n·f. Amplitude modulation by a pulse-like voice wave at less than full depth is the closer model, and it belongs before the tract notches and the bore, since both act on the combined flow. The interval control should favour 3:2 and 5:2 (subharmonic at f/2), then 4:3 (f/3) and 5:4 (f/4), plus unison with a few hertz of detuning for beats; with a 70 Hz drone the voice sits at 105 or 175 Hz.

### An in-breath lasts about 0.2 s, drops the level and switches the timbre

**Measured.** Circular breathing gives a "brief period during inhalation (typically 0.2 s for this player)". "The amplitude falls during the inhalations and is largest during normal playing", and it decreases within each inhalation. Cheek air "supports loud playing for only a fraction of a second", so players often blow "for several seconds" from the lungs and then refill "during a series of three or four alternations" of brief inhalation and normal playing. The change of tract geometry "makes a substantial change in the timbre", "usually unavoidable", and the tract impedance during the inhalation could not be measured because it is too short. As for noise, the jet "experiences viscous drag … which leads to turbulent mixing", and with the tongue low only the strong low harmonics are "clearly visible above the turbulent noise present inside the mouth" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). An artificial player with a simple spring lip is "rather breathy" from turbulence around the valve ([UNSW, sound files](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didj_files.html)). MRI and ultrasound show the mechanism: the tongue seals against the soft palate and then "moves forward like a piston", or "acts as a syringe", pushing mouth air into the instrument while air enters through the nose ([Wiggins and Storey 2010](https://cds.ismrm.org/protected/10MProceedings/PDFfiles/5007_7323.PDF); [Zolotas and Bird 2010](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/view/2313)).

**Authors' interpretation.** The falling amplitude follows the Young–Laplace relation, flatter cheeks giving less pressure for the same muscle tension ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). During the inhalation only "the closed volume of the mouth" stands behind the lips, with "very different resonances" ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)), and the breathing adds "a pulsation to the drone", then "enhanced by rhythmic variations in blowing pressure and vocal tract configuration" ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)).

**Derived in the notes.** A breath event for a synthesizer is about 0.2 s long: the level steps down and then sags further, and the vowel band switches abruptly to a "mouth only" setting and back. It uses about 20–60 mL of cheek air at the measured flow. Noise that originates upstream of the lips passes the same valve, so it should carry the tract's notch pattern, as the injected test noise did, and it is chopped at the drone frequency by lips that are shut half the time; steady pink noise ahead of the vowel filters gets the colouring roughly right and misses the modulation. Pink is an assumption, because nothing found states the noise spectrum.

### The lips can move the drone by about a semitone, and usually sit 2–3 % above the resonance

**Measured.** Across the 38 instruments "the average difference between the frequency of the nominal playing pitch and f1 was 2 %, with the pitch frequency usually slightly above f1" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)); on the PVC pipe the playing frequency was about 3 Hz above the first resonance at about 70 Hz ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Added felt damping lowered the playing pitch ([Schneider et al. 2008](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/AAS2008didj.pdf)).

**Authors' interpretation.** The model uses a playing frequency of "1.03 times the first tube resonance, which is typical for high-tongue playing". The player can shift the lip motion between valve types, "though the total range of variation in the case of the didjeridu is only about a semitone (6 %). Informal observations of competent didjeridu players show that they typically raise the drone frequency when wishing to emphasize formants". A very skilled player can tune the drone so that one harmonic is strongly enhanced, "though this technique is not used in traditional playing" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). "Lipping down and to a less extent lipping up are relatively easy on the instrument" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)). For their analysis the UNSW team allowed a player "plus or minus one semitone" ([Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf)).

**Claimed.** A seller's arithmetic with c = 331.6 + 0.6·t m/s puts a pipe tuned at 20 °C about 3.4 % sharp at 40 °C, "halfway between C and C#" ([Didjshop, Physics of the didgeridoo](https://didjshop.com/physicsDidj.html)). Teachers describe a "drop jaw" that lowers the drone and returns when the jaw closes, more so on instruments with higher back-pressure (snippets: [Didgeridoo Dojo, drop jaw](https://dev.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=13338); [X8 Drums](https://x8drums.com/v/blog/2009/01/beginners-guide-to-didgeridoo.asp)).

**Derived in the notes.** The documented bend is about ±0.5 semitone around a rest point 0.3–0.5 semitone above the passive resonance, with more room downward than upward; slides of a tone or more belong to slide instruments. Coupling the vowel to pitch is realistic: a strong high-tongue vowel goes with a drone raised by up to about 3 %, roughly 50 cents. Since a 2 % change reshuffles which upper harmonics meet bore resonances, a small bend changes the brightness at 1–2 kHz even with the vowel fixed, but only in a model that includes bore resonances there.

## The performance is made of split-second toots, pressure surges and breaths

### The toot sounds 15–19 semitones above the drone, and 16 is a fair default

The toot is the second bore resonance. The table sets the published statements side by side; semitone values are conversions made in the notes.

| Source | Evidence | Stated interval or ratio | Semitones |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf): f2/f1 of 38 termite-made instruments | measured (impedance peaks) | 2.6–3.0 | 16.5–19.0 |
| [Schneider et al. 2007](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/SchneiderEtAl.pdf): one forked instrument, played | measured | 2.83–2.93 | 18.0–18.6 |
| [Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf) | authors' statement | "between about 2.5 and 3 times the drone frequency" | 15.9–19.0 |
| [Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf) | authors' statement | "a ninth to an eleventh" | 13–17 |
| [UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html) | authors' statement | "typically a tenth or an eleventh"; a twelfth on a cylinder | 15–17; 19 |
| [aboriginalart.com.au](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html), after Alice Moyle | observed, second-hand | "a seventh to an eleventh … a tenth seems to be more usual" | 10–17; 15–16 |
| [Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo) | tertiary | "around an 11th" (8:3) | 17 |
| [Ginger Root Rhythms](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm): 43 Yolŋu yidaki, "X drone / Y trumpet" | seller data, octave assumed | mode a minor tenth (2.38) | 12 ×2, 13 ×3, 14 ×4, 15 ×16, 16 ×9, 17 ×8, 19 ×1 |
| Cone equation on Fletcher's three instruments | derived in the notes | 2.85, 2.34, 2.72 | 18.1, 14.7, 17.3 |

**Measured and authors' statements.** The second mode "sounds appreciably below the pitch of the expected musical twelfth" ([Fletcher et al. 2001](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/ISMAdidjpaper.pdf)), and "the greater the flare, the flatter the second mode appears relative to the drone fundamental" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)). Two of Fletcher's papers print the interval as "1.30", "1.43" or "a little less than 1.5 times" the fundamental ([Fletcher 2003, hosted by Didjshop](https://www.didjshop.com/austrAboriginalMusicInstruments.htm)); read as frequency ratios these are wrong for a tube closed at the lips, and 1.5 must not be quoted as the toot ratio. The toot takes "about 4–5 kPa" against 1–2 kPa for the drone and "a much higher lip tension", and a sounded upper mode "will itself be accompanied by its own set of harmonics" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)). A narrow, nearly cylindrical bore makes the higher modes "relatively easy to produce"; a widely flaring one makes them "rather difficult" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). For a cylinder the third resonance lies a major seventeenth above the drone (ratio 5), and real instruments give less ([UNSW, What makes a good didgeridoo?](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/GoodDidj.html)).

**Use.** "Occasional accents are also introduced by momentary leaps to the second mode" ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)); "very brief soundings of the second resonance … are sometimes used to interrupt the low note and to create another complex rhythm", while "'bugling', i.e. rapidly sounding the first, second and third resonance in sequence, is possible, but seems to be very rare in traditional performance" ([Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)). "Even the second mode is only employed for brief accents, and not as a sustained tone" ([Fletcher 1996](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/dij/dij.html)), and overblown notes are "not usual in traditional playing in Western Arnhem Land, though [they are] used in the East" ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). Yolŋu teaching material agrees and adds detail: the toot is made with tighter lips moving into a "p" shape while the tongue pushes air and a pulse comes from the lungs; it appears in almost all Yolŋu songs, "often alternating quickly with the lower drone, to cue changes or endings"; Yolŋu use only the first toot, and higher ones "may result in the giggling of Yolŋu children"; isolated, sustained toots also occur, mid-song or as a call at the start of a song ([Yidaki Story, The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)). A collector states that "only the first overtone note is used in traditional Aboriginal society" ([iDIDJ, Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)).

**Claimed.** Sellers call the toot "trumpet", "horn" or "hoot" and describe it as "bright but still balanced" ([Ginger Root Rhythms](https://gingerroot.com/catalog/yidaki-sold.htm)); a maker says toots on a cylinder "are very high and difficult to reach" and on a cone "lower in pitch, making them easier to play and integrate into rhythms" ([Didgitaldoo cheat sheet](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/27/didge-design-cheat-sheet.html)). Three yidaki on the seller's list give a second trumpet note (E drone / A / F#, C / F / Db, D / F# / D#).

**Derived in the notes.** A default of 16 semitones (ratio 2.52) is inside the real range: toward its low side for termite-made instruments in general, where the impedance data run from 16.5 to 19.0, and a good match for Yolŋu yidaki, where 15, 16 and 17 semitones account for 33 of 43 instruments and 15 is the mode. The physically meaningful range of a toot-interval control is **12 to 19 semitones**, complete cone to cylinder; a cylinder model should use 19. The cone equation shows that a 2.3-fold flare already brings the toot down to a minor tenth. The seller's second toots lie 25–26 semitones above the drone and 8–9 above the first toot, against 27.9 and 8.8 for a cylinder, and the cone equation gives f3/f1 = 4.49 (26.0 semitones) for Fletcher's third instrument, the same pattern. The played interval need not equal f2/f1 exactly, since the drone is played 2–3 % above f1 and the toot's offset is unreported. A brighter toot is plausible physically (higher pressure, harmonics starting at 150–250 Hz where radiation and hearing are better) but rests on reasoning and sellers' words, not on a measured spectrum.

### Yolŋu "hard tongue" playing is a small percussive vocabulary, not a vowel sweep

**Described (Yolŋu teaching material).** The drone is made by "fairly relaxed" lips; many non-Yolŋu players play only this note and vary it with mouth shape, written "weeeooooaaaaaow", whereas Yolŋu shape the mouth only subtly and do not play slow sounds or "swirling high harmonics": "It is all about the rhythm" ([Yidaki Story, The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)). Two tongue positions are used, retroflexed and interdental. *Ḏith-dhu* uncurls the tongue from retroflexed to interdental together with a pulse of air from the belly and gives "a brief hint of the trumpet note"; *dhirrḻ* is the reverse motion, flicking the gum ridge, with a strong air pulse "and often a breath in"; brass-style double and triple tonguing ("taka taka") belongs to non-Yolŋu playing ([Yidaki Story, Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240); same page at [tongue-motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/tongue-motions/)). The quick toot is *ḏup* and the return to the drone *pu*, giving *ḏup-pu* ([Yidaki Story, The Trumpet Note](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/the-trumpet-note/)); Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr's teaching CD adds *dhirrḻ-ḻo* and gives exercises in "slow" and "fast" versions ([Yidaki Story, Hard Tongue Didgeridoo](https://yidakistory.com/?p=95)). A pulse of air from the stomach is "like saying 'ha'"; Yolŋu use only single pulses, simultaneous with the tongue movements, "for aggressive accents", the strongest being the "dith" ("the surge of power"), and a subtle version colours slower notes and the introductory drone, heard as "the subtle flutter in some of the long tones" ([Yidaki Story, Pulses of Air](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/pulses-of-air)). Throat muscles "provide extra pressure and a sort of extra 'wobble' to the sound" ([Yidaki Story, Throat Muscles](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/throat-muscles)). Yolŋu do not sing melodies over the drone; they add "a growly quality to the tone with subtle use of voice along with the rhythm", "almost like grunting along with the 'dhirrl'", and animal sounds belong to particular songs ([Yidaki Story, Adding Voice](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/adding-voice/)). A collector summarises: "Traditional Aboriginal techniques are based on pulsed accents effected through tongue, throat and diaphragm manipulations" ([iDIDJ, Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)). An acoustician heard the same thing as the player saying "unvoiced words such as 'ritoru' or even 'didjeridu', with the final 'u' sound prolonged" ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)).

**Described and claimed (contemporary technique).** Lesson material teaches humming while the lips vibrate, dingo howls, dog barks, kookaburra and cockatoo calls ([Didgeridoo Dojo, Vocals and sounds](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=306); snippets: [kookaburra](https://dev.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=13074), [dingo howl](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=337)), a tongue stop that cuts the drone "for punchy fast rhythms", a vocal "pop" taken with a breath, "drop jaw" and the "wobble" (snippet: [Didgeridoo Dojo, advanced lessons](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/advanced-didgeridoo-lessons-techniques)), and beatboxing merged with the drone (snippet: [Didge Project, beatbox](https://didgeproject.com/beatbox/didgeridoo-beatbox-tutorial-3-essential-rhythms/)). A magazine author describes moving the cheeks "as though you have a mouthful of mouthwash" for a filter sweep ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)), and Wikipedia mentions dingo and kookaburra imitations ([Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)). In eastern Arnhem Land the player may also strike the tube with a stick or flick it with a finger in time with the singer ([aboriginalart.com.au](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** A Yolŋu accent is a compound event: a loudness surge, a fast transit between two extreme tongue positions (a fast formant gesture), and sometimes a momentary flash of the overblown note. The two motions are mirror images, so a pattern needs at least two step "colours". The traditional toot is mostly a very short stab that returns to the drone inside the same rhythmic cell; a toot held as long as a key is the special case. The traditional growl is a rhythm-locked grunt, and sustained sung intervals are the contemporary idiom.

### The breath is part of the rhythm: three-pulse cells and clapstick beats of 46–146 bpm

**Described.** Non-Yolŋu players use the cheeks as a bellows and "play for a while, take a big breath, and then play for a while again"; Yolŋu keep the cheeks in and take "lots of small breaths supported with and initiated by bursts of air from the belly". The placement is exact: "Push air out with the belly right as you begin to say 'dhirrl,' and breathe back in through the nose as the tongue motion is completing"; asked how children learn circular breathing, Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr answered "learn what?" ([Yidaki Story, Breathing](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/breathing)). The base teaching rhythm is "dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl dith-dhu dhirrl dhirrl"; one variation replaces the last *dhirrl* of the first line with a *dith-dhu*, and another does the same with *dup-pu* in place of every *dith-dhu* ([Yidaki Story, Tongue Motions](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=240)). Yolŋu songs have many changes rather than one repeating rhythm (snippet: [Yidaki Story, When, Where and by Who](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/yolngu-rom/when-where-and-by-who)). Teachers of the contemporary style call quick out-in-out-in breathing "bounce breathing" and warn that done too slowly "it doesnt keep the pressure up" ([Didge Project, bounce breathing](https://www.didgeproject.com/circular-breathing/circular-breathing-basics-didgeridoo-bounce-breathing/)); beginner patterns are written with "(B)" for a breath, such as "wee or (B) wee or (B)", with no tempo given (snippets: [Beginner Rhythm 2](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=376), [Beginner Rhythm 3](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=377), [The wobble #3](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/advanced-didgeridoo-lessons-techniques/wobble3)). A collector characterises north-east Arnhem playing as "sharp tongued accents, syncopated overtone rhythms, and variable time metering" ([iDIDJ database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)).

**Authors' interpretation (acoustics).** During the quick nasal inhalation the sound continues but "its timbre changes sharply"; players use this and changes in loudness to mark the rhythm ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)).

**Observed (ethnomusicology).** No tempo was found for Yolŋu playing. Measured clapstick tempos exist for didjeridu-accompanied *wangga* of the Daly region ([Marett, Barwick and Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)):

| Repertory | Slow (bpm) | Moderate (bpm) | Fast (bpm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrtjap | 58–65 | 117–120 | 126–144 |
| Muluk | 50–55 | 110–113 | 126–140 |
| Mandji | 46–48 | 110–116 | 130–140 |
| Lambudju | 64–69; slow moderate 99–107 | 110–116 | 120–125 |
| Walakandha (early) | 60–72 | 112 | 120–136 |
| Walakandha (after 1986) | 55–65 | none listed | 133–142 |
| Ma-yawa | slow moderate 117–125 | 134–146 | none listed |

West Arnhem *manyardi* uses bands of about 50–80 bpm (slow), 81–95 (slow moderate), 96–120 (moderate) and 121–300+ (fast and fast doubled), and its songs last "between one and two minutes" ([ANU Press, Mamurrng ceremony chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)); Marri Ngarr *lirrga* tempos fall into four discrete bands with none between MM 134 and 254 (snippet: [Barwick, lirrga](https://core.ac.uk/download/41240478.pdf)). Rhythmic modes are defined by clapstick tempo and beating pattern (even; uneven triple to sextuple, beats then a rest; doubled; suspended). In the unmeasured mode "the rhythm of the didjeridu is similarly irregular"; with the sticks suspended in slow mode "the didjeridu pulse continues to clearly articulate a regular duple subdivision of the beat" ([Marett, Barwick and Ford, rhythmic modes](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-characteristics-of-the-rhythmic-modes.html)). The didjeridu has "specific patterns associated with each mode", and the singer may sing the required pattern to the player in "didjeridu mouth sounds" before a song ([Marett, Barwick and Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** In the Yolŋu teaching cell two of every three pulses carry an inhalation, so the in-breath timbre recurs at the pulse rate and is a per-step colour, not a once-per-bar event. A duple subdivision of a 46–72 bpm slow beat is 92–144 didjeridu pulses per minute, and doubled beating is roughly 240–290 strokes per minute. The documented patterns are short, asymmetric cells of three pulses paired into six, varied at the line end over a two-line period, and their cells are unequal inside (*ḏith-dhu* and *ḏup-pu* have two parts, *dhirrḻ* one); they are not an even stream of identical steps.

### East Arnhem styles toot, West Arnhem styles do not, and every item starts once and stops hard

**Observed and claimed.** The standard division is "B-type", overtone-present accompaniment in east Arnhem Land, and "A-type", overtone-absent accompaniment in the west (snippet: [Duke University Musical Instrument Collections](https://sites.duke.edu/dumic/instruments/woodwinds/oceania/didgeridoo/)). In the A-type the fundamental gives a continuous drone "with slight pitch inflections" or colouring from a vocalised sound, and the far end is held above the ground; B-type players rest it on the ground or in a resonator, and an ensemble never has more than one didjeridu ([aboriginalart.com.au](https://aboriginalart.com.au/didgeridoo/trad.html)). A collector describes north-central Arnhem playing as overtone-present but "slower and deliberately sustained", Groote Eylandt playing as fast percussive overtones over tongued doublets and triplets, West Arnhem mago as "somewhat richer and more full-bodied" instruments that do not sound the overtone easily, and Daly and Kimberley accompaniment as overtone-absent, "simple and repetitive" ([iDIDJ database](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-database/)). Clans prefer different sounds: one Gälpu instrument in E (157 cm) is praised for "incredibly strong and warm bass response", and a Wangurri instrument in F# (136 cm) is "dirtier", with more midrange ([Yidaki Story, Dhuwa and Yirritja yiḏaki](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=312)). Yolŋu *manikay* is a small group of singers with paired sticks, one yiḏaki and dancers, performed as long series of short items (snippet: [Cultural Survival](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/nurturing-sacred-through-yolngu-popular-song)); the yiḏaki accompanies song and dance and does not play solos ([Yidaki Story, The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)), mainly young men play in ceremony, and some sacred yiḏaki parts are used only in closed ceremony (snippet: [Yidaki Story, When, Where and by Who](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/yolngu-rom/when-where-and-by-who)). Contemporary playing is "swirling, bouncing sounds with plenty of vocal effects", and some Aboriginal players compare it to aeroplane noises ([iDIDJ, Didgeridoo Info](https://www.ididj.com.au/didgeridoo-info/)); it includes slide instruments (the *didjeribone*) and continuous performances of more than 40 and 50 minutes ([Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo)).

Starts and stops follow rules. Yolŋu players "start the yidaki with short notes, as if warming it up", then play a short drone before the rhythm ([Yidaki Story, The Drone](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/?p=237)). Every *wangga* item begins with an instrumental introduction that "establishes both the pitch and the tempo of the song", the didjeridu "always continues throughout the vocal section", and at the end the didjeridu player finishes first, on the singer's signal ([Marett, Barwick and Ford, wangga conventions](https://open.sydneyuniversitypress.com.au/9781920899752/9781920899752-the-music-and-dance-conventions-of-wangga.html)). In *manyardi*, voice and didjeridu finish first, "leaving the clapsticks to beat a final 'terminating pattern'" ([ANU Press, Mamurrng ceremony chapter](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n2451/html/ch01.xhtml)), and *kun-borrk* songs nearly always start with the didjeridu and most commonly end with it stopping first (snippet: [Wikipedia, Indigenous music of Australia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunggul)).

Reference players and recordings named by the sources follow; several of these players have died, and they are named here as the sources name them. For Yolŋu style: *Hard Tongue Didgeridoo* by Milkayŋu Munuŋgurr and Djalu' Gurruwiwi's instructional CDs, with Mirarra Burarrwaŋa, Djakapurra Munyarryun, Mirrwatŋa Munyarryun, Larry Winiwini Gurruwiwi and Mizak Babamiku Gurruwiwi as players to watch, and a warning that the style may not be learnable without Yolŋu teachers in person ([Yidaki Story, Conclusion](https://yidakistory.com/dhawu/playing-the-didjeriduyidaki/conclusion/)). Field recordings: Alice Moyle's 1962–63 CD from north-eastern Arnhem Land (snippet: [AIATSIS](https://aiatsis.gov.au/publication/35854)) and A. P. Elkin's 1949–52 *Tribal Music of Australia* (snippet: [Australian Screen Online](https://aso.gov.au/titles/music/tribal-music-of-australia/notes/)). For West Arnhem *kun-borrk*: David Blanasi, who popularised the name mako, and his grandson Darryl Dikarrna ([Wikipedia, Didgeridoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didgeridoo); snippet: [Wikipedia, Indigenous music of Australia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunggul)). Contemporary: William Barton ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)), Ondrej Smeykal, Zalem Delarbre, William Thoren, Shibaten, Ganga Giri, David Hudson, Mark Atkins, Xavier Rudd and Stephen Kent ([Didge Project, players](https://www.didgeproject.com/artists/didgeridoo-players-you-should-know/)), and Dubravko Lapaine (snippet: [profile](https://and.nmartproject.net/?p=1588)).

**Derived in the notes.** Within one item the drone is not re-attacked; what a keyboard player treats as new notes are tongue and belly articulations on a continuous tone. The part ends in a hard stop on a cue, often after a toot figure, and no source supports a long release tail. A long meditative drone with a slow vowel wander corresponds to contemporary playing, not to any traditional style described here; a West-Arnhem-like preset would disable the overblow.

### Timbre moves constantly, pitch barely moves, and static drones give imitations away

**Described and claimed.** The instrument "usually plays only one note", at constant frequency while the formants move ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)). Playing louder is mostly more diaphragm pressure with lip tension adjusted so the lips keep vibrating; blown too hard, the lips "blow out" and the drone is lost (snippet: [Didgeridoo Dojo, louder and quieter](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=358)). A recordist boosts the upper mids "to bring out the raspiness of the tone", and the same author judged a synthesizer through a wah-wah pedal and a short high-feedback delay to sound "nothing like the real thing", adding that "any electronic approximation is destined to be pretty rough" and that long crossfade loops are needed to "capture the movement of timbre that characterises the digeridoo" ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)). Vendors point the same way: one sample library disclaims any intent "to represent or replace the authentic realism, cultural importance and soul of traditional aboriginal musicianship" (snippet: [Soundiron](https://soundiron.com/products/didgeridoos)), and another sells itself on avoiding "fixed phrases or uncontrollable drones" ([KVR, DidgHybridoo](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/didghybridoo-by-fluffyaudio/details)). An attempt to drive a real didgeridoo with artificial air sources concluded that the buzzing lips "were not appropriately modelled" and the result "did not sound like a didgeridoo" (snippet: [University of Alberta](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/a39522d3-9062-4622-b68d-c97c5e68132a/download/3b03880b-21b7-4b85-995e-69af0df67387)). Strong, clear formants are what takes a human player time to learn ([UNSW, didjeridu acoustics](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/didjeridu.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** Pitch movement in the drone is event-like (an inflection tied to an articulation, a jaw dip, the jump to the toot), not a slow continuous wander; no source describes slow random drift, and none rules out a small one. Loudness and timbre are coupled through the gesture, so an accent should change level and mouth shape at once; whether level alone brightens the tone is unanswered. The recurring complaint about imitations concerns performance, not spectrum.

## One first-order physical model exists; the rest is hobby patches and sample libraries

### The Fletcher model divides each harmonic of the lip flow by lip, tract and bore impedance

**Authors' model.** The only worked physical model treats lungs, glottis, vocal tract, lips and tube as one electrical analogue network, reduced to two input impedances, Z_V just inside the lips and Z_D just outside, "on the two sides of the lip valve" and so in series for the flow U. Lung impedance is almost purely resistive, about 2 MPa·s·m⁻³; the glottis is a series inertance; the tract is two series-resonant circuits plus a glottis and lung branch. The cylinder bore is Z11 = Z22 = −j·Z0·cot(kL) and Z12 = Z21 = −j·Z0·csc(kL) with k = ω/c − jα; the paper also gives the two-port for a flaring truncated cone. The lip is one degree of freedom, m·z″ + (damping)·z′ + m·ω0²·(z − z0) = A·(σ1·p_M + σ2·p_D), with the signs of σ selecting a "sliding door" or "outward-swinging door" valve, and no numerical lip mass, stiffness or damping. The flow equations are p_L − p_M = Z_V·U, p_D = Z_D·U and U = [2(p_M − p_D)/ρ]^½·S(t). The full harmonic-balance solution is proposed but "will not be carried out or discussed further at this time"; what the paper uses is a first-order result for the flow of harmonic n, with R_n + jX_n the value of Z_V + Z_D at that harmonic: |U_n| = 2·p0·s_n / {[(2ρp0)^½ + s0·R_n]² + s0²·X_n²}^½. The flow leaving the open end is about (Z21/Z22) times the input flow, and the radiated power is the radiation resistance times its square. All equations here were restored in the notes from damaged PDF text ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)).

| Model parameter (the paper's Table I, high tongue) | Value |
|---|---|
| Tube length; diameter | 1.3 m; 40 mm |
| Wall-loss coefficient β | 12 |
| Tract resonances (impedance minima) | 1.5 and 2.5 kHz |
| Q of the tract resonances | 10 |
| Tract characteristic impedance (L/C)^½ | 4 MPa·s·m⁻³ |
| Glottal open area; effective length | 20 mm²; 5 mm |
| Blowing pressure in the mouth p0 | 1 kPa |
| Maximum area of lip opening s0 | 50 mm² |
| Playing frequency relative to the first resonance | 1.03 |

The model reproduces two pronounced formants and a deep minimum that agree "very well" with measured high-tongue spectra on a cylindrical pipe, and the odd-to-even transfer at about 1000 Hz. Its authors state its limits plainly: the first-order result "will not be accurate at low frequencies", the conclusions "are certainly not quantitatively accurate", and "the model still requires development if it is to give an accurate quantitative description of the radiated spectrum"; lip motion is assumed, not computed, and no code is published ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf)). Fletcher's simpler 1996 flow model, U ≈ γ·x·(p0 − p)^½ with lip opening x = a0 + a·sin(2πft) and mouthpiece pressure p ≈ R·U, came with the remarks that the constant-R assumption is "not very good" and that a detailed vibrating-lip model "has not yet been attempted" ([Fletcher 1996, PDF](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/journal/1996/1996_24_1_Fletcher.pdf)). Hollenberg 2000 is the only source that ran a time-domain lip simulation, and it was read as an abstract only ([Hollenberg 2000, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1071/PH00100)).

**Derived and hobby work.** Hindle and Posnett built an electro-acoustic instrument, a loudspeaker in a band-pass box driving a real tube, excited by dijj(f, a, t) = p0/R − (p0²/R³)/(a0 + a·sin(2πft))², which they attribute to Fletcher 1996. Their own verdict is that it "replicates very little of the higher formants", has "far less variation" than a human, and that "the high frequencies roll off sharply after 1kHz"; the sound was "very mechanical, exact, and robotic" until they perturbed frequency and lip opening with **two Brownian random LFOs at 0.1·f and 0.01·f**, added a formant oscillator, and modulated coloured noise by the exciter output; a player in their audience asked for more vocalisations ([Hindle and Posnett 2017](https://softwareprocess.es/pubs/hindle2017NIME-didgeridoo.pdf); Pure Data patches at [github.com/abramhindle/diggeridoo](https://github.com/abramhindle/diggeridoo)). An offline Python renderer multiplies that exciter (low-passed at 2500 Hz) by a simulated bore impedance raised to the power 1.2 and by two Lorentzian peaks at speech F1 and F2 with a 100 Hz bandwidth ([Didgitaldoo, didge2wav](https://didgitaldoo.github.io/2026/02/26/didge2wav.html)). A 1994/95 Csound instrument sends a waveshaped 65 Hz sine plus white noise through one resonator at 1000 Hz whose bandwidth wanders randomly ([Csound mailing list](https://ml.csound.1bpm.net/mailinglist.csound/thread/81560)). A 2008 forum thread proposed a waveguide with a band-pass "lip", a pulse-forming non-linearity and two or three parallel band-passes as the tract, and a player there asked for an even/odd harmonic balance control, pitched pulses for air-speed changes, a vocoder for vocalisations and rhythmic inhale noise; no code or values were posted ([KVR forum](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?t=218612)). A 1995 magazine recipe (oscillator, wah-wah, short high-feedback delay) is the one its own author dismissed ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-own-digeridoo)). Two further patches, a Kyma didgeridoo played from a touch controller and a wavetable oscillator under a breath controller, are documented without their synthesis method ([Synthtopia 2010](https://www.synthtopia.com/content/2010/03/13/manta-touch-controlled-kyma-pacarana-didgeridoo/); snippet: [Synthtopia 2016](https://www.synthtopia.com/content/2016/10/15/using-a-breath-controller-with-modular-synthesizer/)), and a PVC "electronic didgeridoo" processes its own acoustic sound and is not a synthesizer (snippet: [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/diy/article/2010-03/electronic-digeridoo)). No didgeridoo model by Malte Kob or Tamara Smyth was found, and no DAFx, ICMC, SMC or JASA paper presenting a real-time physical-model didgeridoo synthesizer.

### Three peaking cuts, a detuned comb and a two-pulse product follow from the published numbers

Everything in this subsection is **derived in the notes** from the equations and values above; none of it is a published didgeridoo design, and none has been validated by listening.

Model one tract impedance peak as a parallel resonance of height R_p and quality Q, and the lip as the resistance R_lip. The flow gain R_lip/(R_lip + Z_V) is then (s² + s·ω0/Q + ω0²)/(s² + s·(ω0/Q)(1 + r) + ω0²) with r = R_p/R_lip, which is exactly a peaking-equaliser cut of −20·log10(1 + r) dB at the centre and an equaliser Q of Q/(1 + r)^½. "Tongue height" becomes one number r per peak, from 0 for a low tongue to about 8–10 for a high one, and three such biquads at about 1.5, 2.1 and 2.8 kHz reproduce the measured structure at roughly 15 multiplies per sample, with coefficients updated at control rate. With r near 8 the notches are wide and the pass bands between them narrow, which is why the result is heard as formants near 1.8 and 2.4 kHz and not as holes. Because R_lip = (2ρp0)^½/s0, a wider lip opening deepens the notches and a higher pressure makes them shallower, which couples timbre to dynamics. No paper gives measured Q values for the tract peaks, so these notch widths rest on the model's Q of 10.

For a lossless cylinder Z21/Z22 = 1/cos(kL), a feedback comb with inverting feedback and a loop delay of 2L/c: 334 samples at 44.1 kHz and 364 at 48 kHz for a 1.3 m tube, first peak at 66 Hz. A loop low-pass following exp(−2·10⁻⁵·β·L·ω^½/d) supplies the wall loss. Tuning the comb 2–3 % below the oscillator reproduces the measured odd-to-even-to-odd migration with no extra logic. A flared bore needs 4–6 parallel resonators at the stretched mode frequencies in place of the comb, with gains falling quickly above the second mode for a "good" instrument; a band-pass at the fundamental is the first of them.

For the source, generate x(t) = max(0, a0 + a·sin ωt) with a closed fraction of arccos(a0/a)/π, about one half when a0 is near zero, and shape it with the closed form of Fletcher's 1996 flow equation, U = (b/2)·[(1 + 4p0/(b·R))^½ − 1] with b = γ²·x²·R. Its large-opening expansion is the Hindle formula, but the exact form has no singularity as the lips close and costs one square root per sample. The kinks at closure need band-limiting (oversampling or a table indexed by closed fraction). For the voice, replace the sine by a second unipolar, partly closed pulse and multiply the two before the notches and the comb; 3:2 and 5:2 then give the octave-down growl by construction. For the breath, modulate part of the noise by the lip flow, as Hindle and Posnett do and as aspiration noise is treated in speech synthesis.

### Waveguide lip-reed models cost a few dozen operations per sample but need four changes

**Published code and papers.** The cheapest established lip-reed model is Cook's, as shipped in STK's `Brass`: per sample, the difference between mouth pressure (0.3 × breath pressure) and bore pressure (0.85 × delay-line output) goes through a biquad "lip" resonator at the note frequency (pole radius 0.997, gain 0.03), is squared and clamped to 1, and gates between mouth and bore pressure before a DC blocker and a delay of 2·fs/f + 3 samples; a "lip tension" controller scales the lip frequency by 4^(2·norm − 1) ([STK Brass.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thestk/stk/master/include/Brass.h); [STK Brass.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thestk/stk/master/src/Brass.cpp)). Julius Smith describes it as "a second-order resonator whose output is squared and hard-clipped" and notes that Cook's one-mass swinging-door model "works surprisingly well" ([PASP, Modeling the Lips and Mouthpiece](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Modeling_Lips_Mouthpiece.html)). Faust's `brassModel` has the same structure, with a lip bandwidth that scales with frequency, a one-pole bell reflection, vowel formant tables with frequency, gain and bandwidth for five formants, and the documented caveat that it can be "unstable or sound poor with bad parameters" ([Faust physmodels.lib](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/master/physmodels.lib)). Formant synthesis in general uses "cascade or parallel second-order filter sections, one per formant" ([PASP, Formant Synthesis Models](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Formant_Synthesis_Models.html)). At the expensive end, a finite-difference brass environment with a mass-spring lip and Bernoulli flow (trumpet values: lip mass 5.37×10⁻⁵ kg, lip frequency 550 Hz, mouth pressure to 5 kPa) needed 0.857 s of computation per second of audio for a 1.3 m trumpet with vectorised code and 3.63 s for a 4.5 m horn on an Intel Core i5-4300U ([Harrison et al. 2015](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/21585566/Harrison_etal_CMJ_2015_An_Articulated_Brass_Instrument_Synthesis.pdf)). Known through summaries only: a trombone model built from two measured transfer functions for interactive use ([Smyth and Scott 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/56375293.pdf); [Scott, MSc thesis](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/12432)), a hobby re-implementation that could not get its mouthpiece model to work ([Trombone Simulator](https://nuchi.github.io/trombone)), Smith's recommendation of a finite-difference lip valve with a waveguide bore and a truncated-IIR bell filter ([J. O. Smith, Brasses](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/jnmr/Brasses.html)), and a linear stability analysis showing that artificial lips have several mechanical resonances ([Cullen, Gilbert and Campbell 2000](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00474993)).

**Derived in the notes.** A didgeridoo port of the Cook structure needs four changes. The lip resonator's Q must not collapse at low pitch: a fixed pole radius of 0.997 is a 42 Hz bandwidth, Q ≈ 5 at 220 Hz but only 1.7 at 70 Hz, so the radius should come from a target Q (0.9990 for Q = 5 at 70 Hz and 44.1 kHz) in a state-variable form. The flat 0.85 loop gain, which happens to equal the β = 12 wall loss at 70 Hz, must become the frequency-dependent loss (0.47 at 1.5 kHz) with a dispersion filter that stretches the modes to f2/f1 of 2.6–3.0; the toot then appears by raising lip frequency and pressure, not by a programmed jump. The tract must load the same flow as the bore: written in wave variables, the pressure difference across the lips combined with the Bernoulli law is a quadratic in U with a closed-form root, so no per-sample iteration is needed, and the upstream side can be a 22-sample waveguide (a 17 cm tract at 44.1 kHz) or two or three lumped resonators, with characteristic impedances of 0.2–1 MPa·s·m⁻³ for the bore, 1.3 for a 20 mm tract and 5.2 for a 10 mm constriction. And the half-cycle closure needs a collision term, whose damping is the model's brightness control according to Hollenberg's abstract. Lip parameters would have to be rescaled from brass values (about 62 times the mass at the same stiffness to move 550 Hz down to 70 Hz), since only s0 and p0 are published for the didgeridoo. The risks are fragility, a start-up transient with a threshold pressure, and a pitch that is an outcome of the model, not an input, which a keyboard instrument can only fix with a tuning table. The notes therefore rate a hybrid as lower-risk: keep the oscillator-driven source for exact pitch and take from the physical model only the flow junction, the tract notches and the bore comb.

### Commercial imitations sample; none found claims a physical model

**Claimed (vendor pages).** Soundiron's *Didgeridoos* holds 16 tuned instruments in 14,250 samples (9.04 GB) with sustains, rhythmic grooves, vowel sounds and beatbox-style staccatos, and lists no formant control, toots or breaths as separate articulations ([KVR, Soundiron Didgeridoos](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/didgeridoos-by-soundiron/details)); its one user review praises "authentic articulations and patterns" without comparing it to a live player ([KVR review](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/didgeridoos-by-soundiron/reviews/9955)). FluffyAudio's *DidgHybridoo* (519 samples, 326 MB) morphs between one player's sampled vowels with a formant filter, a rhythmic modulator of 12 patterns that moves between a start and an end vowel, an intensity and a "growl" control, and claims to be "the first didgeridoo library ever which not relies on sampled phrases or generic uncontrollable drones" ([KVR, DidgHybridoo](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/didghybridoo-by-fluffyaudio/details)). Older or smaller libraries are Tonehammer's (429 samples; snippet: [KVR news](https://www.kvraudio.com/news/tonehammer_releases_didgeridoo_for_kontakt_10941)) and Chiqui Audio's six sampled instruments (snippet: [KVR, South Pacific Vibes](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/south-pacific-vibes---didgeridoo-by-chiqui-audio)). No dedicated hardware instrument and no confirmed physical-modelling preset was found, on the strength of two searches.

**Tools and reference material.** Two open bore-design programs turn a list of cone segments into resonance frequencies and an impedance spectrum: DidgeLab, under a non-commercial licence ([DidgeLab](https://www.github.com/jnehring/didge-lab)), and didgmo, last updated in 2011 ([didgmo](https://didgmo.sourceforge.net/)). The usable reference audio is the UNSW appendix: `AT.wav` (pairs of high-tongue and low-tongue drones), `Circular_breaths_v1.wav` (a sustained note, then alternating playing and inhalations), `LloydVocalisation.wav` (drone with voice at several pitches) and a 1000 frames-per-second lip video ([UNSW, yidaki appendix](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html); mirrored at [phys.unsw.edu.au](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)), with the figures of the experiment paper as targets ([Tarnopolsky et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Tarnopolskyetal.pdf)). AudioSet has a "Didgeridoo" class of about 1,240 clips without spectral annotation (snippet: [AudioSet](https://research.google.com/audioset/ontology/didgeridoo.html)).

**Derived in the notes.** What the products treat as essential, in order of how often it appears, is tempo-locked rhythm, vowel movement, growl and beatbox-style attacks; toots and breath are rarely exposed. A synthesizer with continuous vowel, voice and toot control under one rhythm engine does something the libraries largely do not. The three UNSW files make a practical validation set (notch depth and formant position; the 0.2 s amplitude dip and timbre change; the 35 Hz heterodyne spacing), but all were played on a 1.2 m cylinder, their licence is not stated, and they validate the cylinder case only. DidgeLab or didgmo output gives instrument presets (PVC cylinder, near-cylindrical or flared bore) a defensible basis.

Two readings of the engine correct the brief this report was written from, and both matter for the review. The voice is described as a ring modulator, but the code computes `buzz *= 1 − 0.5·growl + 1.3·growl·sung`, amplitude modulation with a carrier; the objection that ring modulation deletes the drone's harmonics therefore does not apply, and what remains is the missing voice harmonics and the over-modulation at high settings. The rhythm pulse is described as ducking the volume, but `rhythmGain_` is 1 at the start of each division and decays toward 1 − depth, so the accent has the right sign; the notes' objection that an accent is a surge and not a duck applies only to the level between pulses. That sag, with its vowel shift, is in fact the engine's nearest thing to the measured in-breath.

### Nobody has measured onsets, the toot's spectrum, breath noise, radiated level or drift

The gaps fall into six groups, and each one marks a part of the synthesizer that citations cannot settle. **Time behaviour** is the largest: attack time, decay time and envelope shape are unmeasured for the drone and the toot; no source describes the first 100–300 ms of the drone (breath before the buzz, a pitch scoop, a flash of the overtone) or the last moments before the stop; there are no durations in milliseconds for a toot stab, a bark or a tongue stroke, no glide times for the formant band, and one player and one figure behind the 0.2 s inhalation. Amir's 2005 paper "Articulation in the didjeridu" is the one title that promises such data, and it was not obtained. **Spectra and levels**: no spectrum, level or attack of a toot; no table of harmonic amplitudes for a termite-made instrument (the values above are readings of two printed figures, one of them a PVC cylinder); no calibrated radiated sound pressure or power, since the UNSW papers normalise to the strongest component; no directivity; no breath-noise level or spectrum; no levels for voice partials or heterodyne components, the one measured player being a non-expert. **Pitch**: no vibrato rate or depth, no drift over a breath or with warming of the tube beyond a seller's arithmetic, no figure in cents for drone stability or for a drop-jaw bend, no pitch change during the inhalation, and a 10 % figure from Hollenberg known only from an abstract.

**The player's side** is measured for two tongue positions only. There are no frequencies for named shapes (ee, oo, ah) or for the "undulation", no tract impedance below 200 Hz or above 3 kHz or as a function of a continuous tongue position, no measured Q or bandwidth for the tract peaks, no tract data during the in-breath, no direct measurement of the flow waveform, no series of waveform against loudness or lip tension, a harmonic law verified only to 500 Hz, and no lip mass, stiffness, damping or collision parameters. **The instruments** studied are a narrow sample: no measured bore profile of any traditional instrument, no acoustic measurement of the wax mouthpiece, no controlled comparison of wall materials, Q and bandwidth reported for the first peak only, and a quality study built on commercial Queensland instruments that says nothing about what Yolŋu players call a good yidaki. Nearly every quantitative statement in this report comes from one research group (UNSW with ANU and Melbourne) and a handful of players. **Performance** is described but not transcribed: no tempo for Yolŋu playing, no notated didjeridu part that could be read, no detailed account of West Arnhem mako playing, no listening test comparing real and synthetic instruments, and no audio checked by ear in this research, because the examples on the Yolŋu teaching site failed to load.

Several works that would close some of these gaps were not obtained in full. Known from an abstract or a record only: Amir 2004, on the spectra of eight instruments ([abstract](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/1170716.htm)), Hollenberg 2000, with lip parameters and simulated waveforms ([abstract](https://doi.org/10.1071/PH00100)), and Knopoff 1997 ([record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/acde1b09-34e3-4447-8ab0-ac11b67f362c)). Known only as citations in the UNSW papers ([Fletcher et al. 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/Fletcheretal.pdf); [Smith et al. 2007](https://www.didjshop.com/_pdf/Yidaki-3.pdf); [Wolfe and Smith review](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/didjeridu_review.pdf)) or in the notes' search results: Amir 2005, Caussé and colleagues 2004 (eight instruments), Wiggins 1988, Fletcher 1983, Alice Moyle's booklet, Izdebski and colleagues 2012 (endoscopy of the glottis during playing), and the Nature 2005 note itself, which used a traditional instrument.

## Conclusion

The research replaces the picture of a buzzing source followed by a vowel filter with a single divider: each harmonic of the lip flow is divided by the sum of lip resistance, tract impedance and bore impedance. That one expression accounts for formants as gaps, for why players prize tubes that are quiet at 1–2 kHz, for a timbre that follows dynamics (the lip resistance depends on pressure and opening) and for a timbre that follows small pitch changes (harmonics moving on and off bore resonances). For a source-filter synthesizer it also names the cheapest evidence-backed changes: cuts in place of the added formant peaks, with their depth as the "tongue" control; a fundamental resonator tuned 2–3 % flat of the pitch and far narrower than the present one; a second pulse-like valve in place of the sine voice; and a 0.2-second in-breath as an event of its own. All of these are derivations from published numbers, and none has been heard.

The evidence runs out exactly where a keyboard instrument makes its hardest choices: how a note begins and ends, how a toot sounds, how much noise and drift a drone carries. Those can only be settled by analysing recordings, and the three public reference files validate a plastic cylinder, not a flared yidaki, so a traditional-instrument target needs new analysis or the unobtained Amir and Hollenberg papers. The sources also draw a line of style through the feature list: slow vowel wobble, drift and a held overblow describe contemporary playing, while the Yolŋu idiom is articulation, breath and stab on a pitch that does not move. The sources also give reasons for care before calling any imitation traditional: Yolŋu teaching material says that some yiḏaki parts belong to closed ceremony and that animal sounds belong to particular songs, and even a sample-library vendor disclaims representing traditional musicianship.
